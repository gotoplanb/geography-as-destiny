# The Simplified Origin Story Is Itself a Legibility Artifact — "Agriculture Flipped a Switch" Serves the State That Teaches It / Compression Applied to Curriculum Rather Than Monuments

**Source:** Dave, Beta seminar, 2026-11-02, reading James C. Scott, *Against the Grain* (Yale, 2017).
**Type:** connection / framework extension
**Relevant to:** Chapter 2 (The Anti-Hero), Chapter 11 (process as evidence), card 347 (monuments as high-compression media), cards 344 and 345 (appropriability and exit), the Scott literature-review entry

## The move

Scott's target is the conventional sequence — agriculture arrives, surplus follows, cities follow, states follow, and the whole thing reads as progress. He argues it is wrong on timing and wrong on mechanism.

Dave's addition asks the next question: **not whether the simplified story is wrong, but why that particular wrong story persists.** His answer: simple narratives are useful to state formation and control in the same way a legible harvest is. A population that believes agriculture flipped a switch toward enlightenment has a tidy account of why the arrangement it lives under is the natural endpoint. A population holding the multi-layered version — millennia between domestication and states, states as frequently escaped from, "barbarians" often refugees — has a story that invites the question of whether the arrangement was ever a good deal.

He pairs this with a non-cynical second mechanism: heuristics are biologically adequate. Simple models are what small groups and individual decision-making actually need, and grade-school versions of every subject are simplified because the learner cannot yet hold the useful version. Both things are true at once, and the card should keep both — the simplification is overdetermined, which is exactly why it is durable.

## Why this matters

**It applies Scott's own legibility argument to history itself.** Scott says the state simplifies the terrain it governs — standardized measures, surnames, cadastral maps, monocrop fields — because what cannot be counted cannot be taxed. Dave extends this to the past: the state also simplifies *its own origin account*, and for the same reason. An illegible history is as inconvenient as an illegible field.

**It is the same mechanism as card 347, on different material.** That card has monuments as "high-compression historical media," where compression is not necessarily a lie but throws information away. The curriculum version is the same operation at larger scale — the Tippecanoe inscription and the agricultural-revolution chapter are both lossy encodings that happen to drop the contested part. Two instances of one mechanism is the beginning of an argument rather than an observation.

**It strengthens the anti-great-man commitment from an unexpected direction.** Chapter 2 argues individuals are outliers on a distribution terrain drew first. This supplies a reason the opposite view is taught: the great-man story and the agriculture-as-switch story are both compressions that survive because they are *transmissible*, not because they are accurate. The book currently asserts the historiographical error. This proposes a selection mechanism for it.

## Challenges to this, which Dave should answer before it goes anywhere

- **The functional claim needs a mechanism, or it is just-so.** "Simple stories serve state control" is satisfying and currently unevidenced. Nobody designed the grade-school account. What is the actual transmission path — curriculum standardization, textbook economics, the structure of examinations? Without one this is the same shape of argument the book refuses elsewhere: a tidy functional explanation standing in for a causal one.
- **It is close to unfalsifiable as stated.** If the simplified story serves the state, and a complicated story would also serve it by demonstrating sophistication, the claim predicts nothing. What would a state teaching an *inconvenient* origin story look like, and does any?
- **The biological-heuristic leg may be sufficient on its own.** If simplification is fully explained by pedagogy and cognitive load, the state-interest leg is redundant. Occam cuts against the more interesting version. The card is only worth keeping if the state leg does work the pedagogy leg cannot — for example, if the *specific content* dropped is reliably the content unfavourable to the state, rather than just the complicated content.

That last one is the testable version and the one worth pursuing. It is checkable: compare what the standard account omits against what would be politically inconvenient, and see whether the overlap exceeds what difficulty alone predicts.
