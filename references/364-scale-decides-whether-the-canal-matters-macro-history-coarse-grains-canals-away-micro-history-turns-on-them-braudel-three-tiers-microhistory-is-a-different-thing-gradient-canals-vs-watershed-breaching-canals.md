## Who has defined this already

**Fernand Braudel is the precedent, and the project already claims him.** *La Méditerranée* (1949) is built on three tiers of time:
- *geographic time*: the near-motionless history of terrain and climate, the **longue durée**;
- *social time*: economies, states and societies over decades to centuries (*conjonctures*);
- *individual time*: events (*l'histoire événementielle*), which Braudel called surface disturbances carried on the tides beneath (paraphrased from memory; check the preface for the exact wording).

The Chapter 1 stub already names Braudel as "the intellectual ancestor" and asks (open question 2) whether he gets a named influence or a footnote. Card 318 lays out the same stack and notes that no Braudel card exists. This card is that card.

**A terminology warning: "microhistory" is taken.** *Microstoria* is a specific Italian school of the 1970s and 1980s:
- Carlo Ginzburg, *The Cheese and the Worms* (1976): the cosmology of one sixteenth-century miller;
- Giovanni Levi, *Inheriting Power* (1985).

It means reducing the *scale of observation* to a single person or village, not studying a shorter timescale. If the book says "micro history" for canals and states, readers who know the field will hear Ginzburg. Use Braudel's vocabulary, or **event scale / structural scale**, instead.

**Other neighbours:**
- David Christian, *Maps of Time* (2004): Big History, macro taken to cosmological scale.
- Jo Guldi & David Armitage, *The History Manifesto* (2014): a recent argument for returning to the longue durée.

## What Dave adds: the canal is a scale probe

Braudel sorts phenomena by *how fast they change*. Dave's version sorts them by **whether they survive coarse-graining**, and the canal is the test object.

**Thermodynamics is literally this distinction.** It is the coarse-grained description of a system in which individual molecular trajectories wash out and only the aggregate quantities (gradient, pressure, flow) remain. The molecules still determine where any single particle goes; they just don't appear at the macro level. That is the book's probabilistic frame stated in physics:
- **the distribution is macro history:** geography sets it, and canals don't move it;
- **the draw is event-scale history:** canals, dams and individuals decide which outcome comes up, when, and who wins.

Card 318's wording is the same: individual action is "the fastest layer that selects the specific draw."

**It resolves cards 362 and 363 without contradicting either:**
- **Card 362 is the macro reading.** Natural rivers give the spine, and canals follow the gradient (card 012), so at the structural scale the canal is redundant with the river.
- **Card 363 is the event-scale reading.** Across the 4.2 ka drought, canals plausibly sorted *which* state fell *when*: Ur III rose while Akkad and Egypt's Old Kingdom fell. At the structural scale the outcome was the same everywhere: the region's states failed or rotated, the people stayed, and new rulers took over the same land. The canal changed the order of the draws, not the distribution.

This is a better reading of 363 than "the prediction failed." The over-optimization claim is an event-scale claim, and it should be tested at that scale.

## Where the clean split breaks: some canals move the distribution

"Canals are irrelevant at the macro scale" holds for canals that **follow a gradient**, which accelerate water already going that way. It does not hold for canals that **breach a watershed or an isthmus**. Those can pass card 361's test by changing who the terrain serves:

| Canal | Kind | Scale it acts at |
|---|---|---|
| Southern Mesopotamian irrigation | Follows the river gradient | Event scale (timing, which city wins) |
| Kissimmee straightening, 1960s (card 012) | Follows the gradient and over-straightens it | Event scale; local ecological damage |
| Grand Canal, Sui, 605–610 CE (card 226) | Links two basins (Yellow River, Yangtze) | **Structural**: southern grain feeds a northern capital for over a millennium |
| Suez (1869), Panama (1914) (card 147) | Cuts an isthmus | **Structural**: friction collapse; reorders the sea routes |

The rule that falls out: **infrastructure belongs to macro history when it creates a connection the terrain didn't offer. It belongs to event-scale history when it speeds up one the terrain already made.** That is the friction-collapse test (card 361) applied to hydraulics, which ties Dave's scale distinction to the framework's existing machinery rather than leaving it as an impression.

## Calibration

- **Dave's "squishy" is right, and that's fine.** Braudel's tiers are bands, not borders. The probe gives a criterion without pretending to be a cutoff: an object is structural if removing it changes the distribution across many draws.
- **Name the magnitude.** Structural means millennia to centuries; event scale means decades to years. Card 351's discipline applies, and a bare "macro" should carry a timescale with it.
- **Even the Grand Canal is arguable.** Card 226 calls it "substrate-aligned": it materialized a reunification pressure that already existed. On that reading it, too, accelerated an existing gradient, just one that is political rather than hydrological. The test still sorts it, but the argument has to be made each time.
