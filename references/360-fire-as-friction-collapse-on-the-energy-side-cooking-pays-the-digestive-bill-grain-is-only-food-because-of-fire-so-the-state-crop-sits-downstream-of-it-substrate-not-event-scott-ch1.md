# Fire as Friction Collapse on the Energy Side — Cooking Pays Part of the Digestive Bill / Grain Is Only Food Because of Fire, So Scott's State Crop Sits Downstream of It / A Substrate, Not an Event

**Source:** Dave, Beta seminar, 2026-11-04, Scott, *Against the Grain* (Yale, 2017), Ch. 1 — domestication, fire, niche construction. Dave proposed fire as a proto-friction collapse: it turned foods of zero or negative caloric value into valuable ones, and it reshaped the land. Beta drew the distinction between the energy side and the movement side, brought in Wrangham and Aiello & Wheeler, and judged it "probably not a card yet." Alpha files it as a **stub**. Two connections to existing cards earn it a place; the rest is held loosely.
**Type:** stub / connection — not load-bearing
**Relevant to:** cards 344 (appropriability), 349 (cheap calories), 301 (caloric thermodynamics), 299 (agriculture as opportunity cost), 351 (order-of-magnitude contrast); Chapter 1 substrate

## What Dave and Beta established

- **Fire collapses a cost, but not a movement cost.** The horse, wagon, sail and railroad collapse the cost of crossing terrain. Cooking collapses the cost of extracting energy from what is already in reach. It is external digestion: part of the metabolic bill is paid before the food reaches the gut. Beta's distinction is worth keeping. It is the cost-to-extract half of the project's cost thread (344, 349) applied to the body rather than to the state.
- **Niche construction** (the term is Odling-Smee, Laland & Feldman, *Niche Construction: The Neglected Process in Evolution*, Princeton, 2003) covers the other half: landscape burning reshapes vegetation to concentrate game and useful plants. The Australian case is the classic one — Rhys Jones, "Fire-stick farming," *Mankind* 7 (1969); Bliege Bird et al., *PNAS* (2008), on Martu burning.
- **Fire widens where the distribution has any mass at all.** Warmth, light and protection from predators extend the habitable range into cold terrain. That is a geography-lens effect: it enlarges the board rather than moving a piece.

## What Alpha adds

**1. Grain is only food because of fire — so the state crop sits downstream of it.** Raw cereal grains are barely digestible. They become a caloric staple only once they are cooked, or ground and cooked. Card 344 makes grain the appropriable staple: cheap to see and cheap to seize, so it is the crop states form around. That presupposes grain is food at all, which it is only after fire. The chain runs: **fire makes grain edible → grain becomes a staple → the staple is appropriable → the state.** Fire does not *select* grain over tubers, since many tubers also need cooking and cassava is toxic raw. It is the floor under both. But it is the oldest link in Scott's own causal chain, and he appears to put it in Ch. 1 for that reason. **Check:** does Scott draw the grain–fire link explicitly, or is this the project's inference?

**2. Name the magnitude: this is a substrate, not an event.** The dates sit on a scale of **hundreds of thousands to about two million years**:
- Wrangham's habitual cooking at around 1.8 million years.
- Wonderwerk Cave at around a million (Berna et al., *PNAS*, 2012).
- Gesher Benot Ya'aqov at around 790,000 (Goren-Inbar et al., *Science*, 2004). Beta did not mention it, but it is the strongest early claim between Wonderwerk and the later evidence.
- Habitual use solid in Europe only from around 400,000 (Roebroeks & Villa, *PNAS*, 2011).

The book's causal register runs in centuries and millennia. Fire sits two or more orders of magnitude below it, in the same position as terrain: a condition the distribution is drawn on, not a draw. That is why it should not become a chapter episode, and why "outside the scope" is half right. It is outside the narrative but inside the substrate. Card 351's order-of-magnitude discipline applies.

**3. Diffusion is real, and so is loss.** Beta is right that fire is natural but making and keeping it is a technology. The sharper diffusion point is that the technology could be *lost*. Sandgathe, Dibble et al. (*Journal of Human Evolution*, 2011) argue that Neanderthals at Pech de l'Azé IV and Roc de Marsal used fire *less* in colder phases, when lightning-lit fire was scarcer. That suggests some populations relied on natural fire rather than making it. If that holds, fire-making is a classic diffusion case: an innovation with a natural substitute, adopted unevenly, and dropped when the substitute was available. **Contested — verify before relying on it.**

## Corrections to the exchange

- **The chimp gut "correction" wasn't one.** Dave said the chimp gut is proportionally larger; Beta restated the same thing as "it's our gut that's reduced." The real problem is the figure. **"Three times bigger" is unverified and should not stand.** The documented claim is Aiello & Wheeler, "The Expensive-Tissue Hypothesis," *Current Anthropology* 36 (1995): the human gut is roughly 60% of the mass expected for a primate of our body size. The large difference with chimps is in gut *proportions*, with chimps' larger colon. That is a different quantity from Dave's stomach ratio. Use Aiello & Wheeler's framing rather than a multiplier.
- **Wrangham's 1.8-million-year date is his inference from anatomy** — the shrinking teeth and gut of *Homo erectus*. It is not archaeology. The gap between the anatomical inference and the hearth evidence is the actual dispute, and the Wonderwerk and Gesher Benot Ya'aqov dates sit inside it.
