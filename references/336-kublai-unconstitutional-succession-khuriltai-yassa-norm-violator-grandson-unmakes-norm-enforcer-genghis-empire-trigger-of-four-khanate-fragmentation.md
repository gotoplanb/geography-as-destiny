## The "constitution" — answered
There was **no written Mongol constitution.** Cunliffe's "unconstitutional" is shorthand for violating the **customary succession law**: the **khuriltai** (*kurultai* — the great assembly of Mongol nobility that convened in the ancestral lands to elect/confirm the Great Khan by consensus of the eligible lineages) plus the **Yassa/Jasagh** (the code of law attributed to Genghis). The decisive fact: **Genghis himself decreed that any future Great Khan could be chosen *only* by a khuriltai, not by familial succession alone** — so the succession norm was **Genghis's own codified law.** (Verify Cunliffe's exact word; the underlying event is certain, the term is his gloss.)

## What Kublai actually did (and the counterintuitive part)
Möngke Khan died in 1259 with no declared successor. A proper **khuriltai at Karakorum** (the capital, the ancestral lands, with the traditional lineage representation) proclaimed **Ariq Böke** Great Khan "in the traditional Mongolian style." **Kublai instead declared himself Great Khan on 5 May 1260 at Kaiping (Shangdu) — his *Chinese*-style city — rather than trekking to Karakorum for a full assembly.** Ariq Böke's party denounced Kublai's election as *irregular*: only a kurultai in the ancestral lands with full lineage representation could make a rightful ruler. **So the celebrated founder of the Yuan (Kublai) was the norm-*breaker*; Ariq Böke was the "constitutional" claimant.** History remembers the winner; the winner broke the law.

## The bookend (Dave's poetic point, made structural)
This is the empire's **norm-fidelity → norm-violation bookend**, and it is exact:
- **Genghis BUILT the empire on norm-*fidelity*.** Card 118's whole argument: Genghis followed every Silk Road commercial/diplomatic norm in sequence; his violence was norm-*enforcement*. And he did more than enforce — he **codified** the succession norm (the Yassa/khuriltai). The empire's cohesion *was* its norms, enforced and written down by the founder.
- **Kublai UNMADE it by norm-*violation* — of his grandfather's *own* law.** Seizing the Great Khanate without a proper khuriltai broke precisely the succession rule Genghis had decreed. **The grandson who "won" (founded the Yuan, ruled China) is the one who broke what the grandfather built** — his personal triumph and the empire's fracture are *the same act.* The norm-enforcer's empire was undone by a norm-violator from inside his own bloodline, breaking the norm the enforcer himself had laid down. Poetic *and* mechanistic: **norms are the connective tissue; the founder who enforced them coalesced the system, the heir who violated them fractured it.**

## The mechanism — succession norm as failed *legitimating* middleware (extends 217)
Card 217: you can unify by force, but you can only *hold* by legitimating ideology/middleware. **The khuriltai was the Mongol Empire's legitimating-succession middleware — the one mechanism that could produce a Great Khan with *empire-wide* legitimacy across the four uluses.** Kublai's violation didn't just start a war; it **destroyed the legitimacy-generating mechanism itself.** After the **Toluid Civil War (1260–1264)**, no Great Khan could command empire-wide legitimacy again — Kublai "won," but as an *irregular* Khan the western khanates never fully recognized. The succession norm's collapse is 217's "legitimating sustainment" failing at the succession joint, and it is why unified authority never recovered.

## Proximate trigger on a geographically-overdetermined substrate (holds 120/241)
The framework already reads the **four-khanate fragmentation as geographically overdetermined** — each ulus sat on distinct terrain and drifted onto its own cultural-political trajectory (Golden Horde, Chagatai, Ilkhanate, Yuan; cards 120/241 — "geographic determinism confirmed four times," the cultural-gravity conversion clock). Dave's point supplies the **proximate trigger**: the Kublai succession crisis is *when and how* the geographically-latent fragmentation **ignited.** This is the framework's standard **contingent-trigger-on-a-substrate-set-distribution** move (An Lushan, 317/230): the fragmentation was terrain-overdetermined (the substrate/kindling); Kublai's norm-violation is the **spark** that set it off and made it irreversible. So the geographic reading (120/241) **holds** — this doesn't replace it; it names the ignition event the deep cause was going to find sooner or later. (By Kublai's death in 1294 the empire had settled into the four separate polities.)

## The Song reprieve (a concrete consequence)
When Möngke died (1259), the Mongol **campaign against the Southern Song was abandoned** as the succession scramble consumed the leadership; it was not seriously resumed until after the civil war. The Song imperial family surrendered in 1276 and the last loyalists fell only in **1279.** So the conqueror's succession crisis **bought the target ~a decade** — internal norm-violation abroad becomes external reprieve. (Dave's "ten years of civil war" is the *extended* Mongol infighting/fragmentation period; the main Toluid Civil War was ~4 years, 1260–64, with the Kaidu–Kublai war running on afterward — see calibration.)
