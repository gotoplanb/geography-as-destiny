## The correction (fix in-card, per "verify, don't punt")
1. **Two Chandraguptas — the Jain-abdication figure is Chandragupta *Maurya*, not the Gupta dynasty.** Chandragupta **Maurya** (r. ~321–297 BCE), founder of the Mauryan Empire (defeated the Nandas with Kautilya/Chanakya; the *Arthashastra* statecraft tradition; treaty with Seleucus ~305 BCE; Megasthenes at Pataliputra), is the one traditionally associated with the Jain abdication. The **Gupta** dynasty — Chandragupta **I** (~320 CE) and **II** — is a *different dynasty ~600 years later*. Cunliffe is discussing the **Mauryan** Chandragupta (the post-Alexander Indian power on the Eurasian stage); Dave's "Gupta" is the shared-name conflation. Verify Cunliffe's exact wording, but the abdication detail belongs to the Maurya.
2. **Jainism did not begin during the Guptas *or* the Mauryas.** It traces to **Mahavira** (~6th–5th c. BCE, the 24th Tirthankara) and the older Tirthankara/**śramaṇa** ascetic tradition (Parshvanatha, the 23rd, traditionally ~8th c. BCE). That is **~2 centuries before Chandragupta Maurya** and **~800+ years before the Guptas.** So "did Jainism begin during Gupta?" — emphatically no; it was already an established tradition.
3. **The abdication is late, semi-legendary, and contested — softer than it looks.** The Chandragupta-becomes-Jain-monk-at-Shravanabelagola-and-dies-by-*sallekhana* story comes from **Digambara Jain accounts written 1,200+ years after his death** (Harishena's *Brihatkatha-kosa*, 931 CE; Ratnanandi's *Bhadrabahu-charita*, ~1450 CE). The Shravanabelagola inscription does **not** name Chandragupta-the-emperor, and a serious scholarly position holds the abdication/*sallekhana* actually belongs to his descendant **Samprati** (Ashoka's grandson), not Chandragupta himself. **Treat the abdication as tradition, not documented fact.** (The associated 12-year-famine tale — Bhadrabahu's prophecy, migration south — is part of the *same* legendary cluster, framed karmically as the fruit of the conquests' violence, not as a materially-documented environmental cause.)

## Dave's instinct, assessed — a real exception to MODE 3
The framework already names the pattern Dave describes ("rulers adopt religion because it's useful substrate for statecraft/stabilization"): it is **MODE 3 — religion as a deliberate state instrument / legitimating ideology** (cards 212/216/217/220; the canonical case is Confucianism elevated under Han Wudi/Dong Zhongshu, and **Ashoka's Buddhism is already a table entry in 212**). Dave is right that **voluntary abdication to become an ascetic is categorically different**: it is religion **overriding** statecraft, not **serving** it. So — *if historical* — it is a **genuine exception to MODE 3**, and the framework should hold it as such rather than forcing it into the instrumental mold. Not everything is MODE 3; a clean exception is worth having on record (Beta's point, endorsed).

## But the exception still sits on framework-legible substrate (the productive part)
The framework doesn't explain the *personal* choice — but it explains the *field* the choice was made in, on three legs:

- **The religious *menu* was substrate-produced.** The renunciatory, *ahimsa* (non-violence) traditions Chandragupta could turn to — Jainism, Buddhism, the broader śramaṇa movement — arose from the **second urbanization of the Gangetic plain (~600 BCE)**: iron-age cities, coinage, long-distance trade, and a rising **merchant/urban class** for whom non-violence and personal soteriology suited commerce better than the Brahmanical **warrior-and-blood-sacrifice** order. So the framework's claim isn't "geography made Chandragupta abdicate"; it's "**the substrate produced the menu of renunciatory ahimsa options that existed to be chosen.**" (This merchant-śramaṇa substrate reading is itself an **uncarded gap** — worth its own card.)
- **Anti-great-man: a durable institution lets even the founder exit.** The Mauryan state (Kautilyan/*Arthashastra* bureaucracy) was robust enough that the **founder could leave and the empire not merely survive but *expand*** — Bindusara held it, Ashoka expanded it to near-subcontinental scale. Dave's own inference — "minimal external pressure, or the son and grandson couldn't have held power" — points exactly here: the stable succession is evidence of a **durable institutional substrate**, not necessarily of a pressure-free heart. The institution's durability is *what makes a founder's exit survivable* (the 217/225 "institution survives the founder" leg — Qin's bureaucracy outlasting the dynasty; Constantine saving the substrate, not himself).
- **Chandragupta (personal renunciation) vs. Ashoka (instrumental MODE-3 dhamma) — one dynasty, both modes.** The founder renounces power for asceticism (the *exception*); two generations later the grandson deploys **Buddhist dhamma as imperial legitimating ideology while ruling** (canonical MODE 3, already in 212's table, post-Kalinga). The same dynasty displays both the rule and its exception — a clean teaching contrast. **The Ashoka half is developed in its own card — see 329** (Kalinga→Dhamma as the self-documented "hold by legitimacy, not sword" case; legitimacy cheaper than coercion). (Held loosely: a recurring "blood-cost-of-conquest → religious turn" motif links them — Chandragupta's Jain turn from conquest-guilt in the legend, Ashoka's dhamma from Kalinga-remorse — but the Chandragupta half is legendary, so don't lean on the motif as established.)

## Why this matters
- **Keeps MODE 3 honest.** The framework's instrumental-religion pattern is strong *because* it admits genuine exceptions rather than swallowing every case. Voluntary renunciation is the exception; naming it sharpens the rule.
- **Shows the framework still has purchase on the exception** — not on the personal choice, but on the substrate-produced menu and the founder-survivable institution. "The framework doesn't explain *why he chose*, but it explains *why those were the options* and *why the state didn't fall when he left*."
- **Opens two adjacent gaps worth filling:** (a) a **Mauryan-statecraft** card (Chandragupta/Kautilya/Ashoka — currently only a table entry in 212, genuinely new territory), and (b) a **merchant-urban śramaṇa-substrate** card (ahimsa as trade-compatible ethics vs. the Brahmanical warrior-sacrifice order — the framework's apparatus generates it but it isn't carded).

## Calibration / open items
1. **Don't build on the abdication as fact.** It is late Digambara tradition (1,200+ yr after), the inscription doesn't name the emperor, and it may belong to Samprati. The framework points above hold **regardless** of whether the specific abdication is historical — which is exactly why this doesn't need a GitHub issue: nothing load-bearing rests on it.
2. **Don't over-read the famine as environmental forcing.** The 12-year-famine tale is part of the same legend and is framed *karmically* (guilt over the violence of conquest), not as a documented material trigger. Resist the temptation to make it a clean climate-forcing case.
3. **The merchant-śramaṇa substrate and the Mauryan-statecraft engagement are gaps, not claims made here.** This card flags them; it doesn't develop them. Keep the ahimsa-suits-trade reading as a *plausible substrate hypothesis* pending a dedicated card and sourcing.
4. **Verify Cunliffe's actual treatment** — which Chandragupta, and what he says about the succession mechanics — before any prose. Dave's "minimal external pressure" is a reasonable inference from the stable succession, but the abdication's historicity is the prior question.
5. **This is a correction + exception-framing card, not a Mauryan corpus.** Cross-reference the MODE-1/2/3 cluster (209/212/216/217/220); don't re-derive it.
