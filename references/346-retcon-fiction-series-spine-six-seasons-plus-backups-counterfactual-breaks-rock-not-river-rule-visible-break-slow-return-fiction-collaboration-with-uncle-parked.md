**Source:** Dave, 2026-10-08. Origin: his uncle, having read the book, called with the idea of a novel about Yalta going differently. Dave generalised that single counterfactual into the series spine below. Season 5 is therefore the seed, not a derived season. Spine filed verbatim.
**Type:** project idea / adaptation
**Relevant to:** not the dissertation — a separate fiction project built on the framework. Season mapping to chapters below.

## Status

Parked. This is a place for the idea to live, not a worked treatment. Nothing here has been researched, costed, or tested against the historical record; the counterfactual premises are dramatic propositions, not claims the framework endorses. Treat every date and name below as unverified until someone checks it.

Worth noting what the structure is doing, because it is not incidental: the series rules are a dramatization of the book's central commitment. Rule 2 ("if removing the rock still gets you the same outcome within a generation, it was honest") is the falsifiability test from Chapter 1 restated as a writers'-room constraint, and Rule 3's visible-break-slow-return is the probability distribution reasserting itself. The anti-great-man position survives the translation to fiction, which is the hard part — individuals get to matter at the scene level while the river still wins.

## The spine, as written

*A fiction series built on the physics of the book. Each season is one counterfactual "break" in history. The break changes a human draw (who decides what) and a friction (what the terrain allows). The seasons then show the river drifting back toward the historical channel, without arriving.*

### Series Rules

1. **Predictable physics, unpredictable people.** Terrain and logistics stay consistent. Drama comes from choices inside the constraints.
2. **Every break starts with a rock, not a river.** The trivial cause has to sit in a riverbed that already wanted the outcome. Test: if removing the rock still gets you the same outcome within a generation, it was honest.
3. **Visible break, slow return.** The opener shows a change the audience can see immediately. The middle episodes show restoring forces. The final episode shows the first faint signs of drift, never completion.
4. **Anti-cliffhanger endings.** The last episode ends on a question about the terrain, not a resolution. At least one restoring force fails, so the drift looks like a tendency, not a plan.
5. **Diffusion follows Rogers' compatibility.** An innovation spreads where it fits the adopter's existing substrate, and is reshaped where it doesn't.

### Season 1: The Paper Court
**Setting:** Sasanian Persia, 7th century

- **Break:** The Sasanians adopt paper for administration decades before the Arab conquest. The Persian chronicle becomes the dominant record of the period.
- **Micro draw:** A provincial fiscal official decides to switch from parchment to paper to cut costs.
- **Friction:** A record-keeping state can hold its provinces together through the crisis of 630–650. The Arab wave meets a government that negotiates rather than collapses.
- **Rock:** A Chinese papermaker captured at a frontier skirmish is brought to a provincial workshop, and the fiscal official sees the savings.
- **Opening image:** A line of a surviving text, read as a fragment, with the rest of the chronicle missing.
- **Drift:** Paper reaches the Islamic world through this court rather than Samarkand. Scribal traditions take on the familiar Abbasid shape, in a different accent.
- **Closing image:** A scribe begins a new chronicle while Arab caravans pass through the border towns. The record hasn't caught up with the river.

### Season 2: The Compass Gulf
**Setting:** Persian Gulf and Indian Ocean trade, 11th century

- **Break:** The compass is adopted for maritime navigation in the Gulf and Indian Ocean before it reaches the Mediterranean.
- **Micro draw:** A Gulf pilot chooses to sail open water by compass rather than follow the coast.
- **Friction:** Gulf merchants gain a reliable sea route that bypasses the Red Sea and the caravan towns. Wealth pulls toward the Gulf.
- **Rock:** A Song-dynasty geomancy instrument used for siting buildings is traded to a Gulf pilot, who sees its use at sea.
- **Opening image:** A harbor crowded with ships whose routes don't match the coastal charts.
- **Drift:** Mediterranean powers adopt the compass through their own channels. The Gulf route settles back into familiar corridors, with different ports at the center.
- **Closing image:** A merchant's ledger shows the Gulf route's profits, while the old caravan towns quietly keep their trade contracts.

### Season 3: The Buffer
**Setting:** Northern Song and Jurchen, 1115–1125

- **Break:** The Northern Song does not arm the Jurchen against the Liao.
- **Micro draw:** The Song minister chooses diplomacy over the alliance that historically destroyed the Liao buffer.
- **Friction:** The Liao keep absorbing steppe pressure on the Song's behalf. The Jin never destroy the buffer and never reach Kaifeng.
- **Rock:** A tribute dispute with the Liao is resolved by an envoy's negotiation, not by a military gamble.
- **Opening image:** A border town where the tribute caravans still arrive on schedule.
- **Drift:** Pressure finds the boundary eventually. The Song have a generation to prepare, and the Southern hills begin to act as the bumper.
- **Closing image:** A Song garrison commander stares north at a steppe horizon that looks quieter than it should.

### Season 4: The Tollbooths
**Setting:** Pacific, 1893–1898

- **Break:** The 1893 Hawaii coup fails, and Cleveland's investigation restores the monarchy.
- **Micro draw:** The U.S. president's decision to back the restoration, rather than the sugar planters.
- **Friction:** Hawaiian control of the mid-Pacific waystation changes the map but not the gradient. The Manila Galleon logic still points toward the Pacific chokepoints.
- **Rock:** A ship's captain refuses a coaling order, and the diplomatic incident forces a rethink of the naval calculus.
- **Opening image:** A Honolulu harbor where the flag over the palace is different.
- **Drift:** A Pacific acquisition happens elsewhere in the chain. The tollbooth gets built, under a different flag.
- **Closing image:** A surveyor's report recommends a coaling station at an island the story hasn't named yet.

### Season 5: The Elbe Line
**Setting:** Europe, 1944–1945

- **Break:** Roosevelt dies before Yalta. The Western armies keep moving east in April 1945 instead of halting at the Elbe.
- **Micro draw:** Eisenhower (or his successor) decides to push on toward Berlin rather than pause for the Soviet meeting.
- **Friction:** The Western front reaches further east. The Soviet logistics ceiling still sits near the Rhine, and the Channel still holds.
- **Rock:** A supply officer's routine request for trucks is delayed a week, and the Western timetable shifts.
- **Opening image:** A map table with a line drawn a few hundred kilometers further east than history recorded.
- **Drift:** A divided Germany still appears, with its border somewhere else. Britain stays the island, the bumper that holds the Atlantic open.
- **Closing image:** A railway station on the new border where the gauge changes, and the cargo is still unloaded by hand.

*Audience note:* This is the season for mass audiences. The historical anchors are familiar, and the break is easy to recognize.

### Season 6: The Medicine Lodge
**Setting:** Southern Plains, 1867–1875

- **Break:** The 1867 treaty is read by both parties as a living relationship instrument, not a self-executing contract.
- **Micro draw:** A treaty translator flags the encoding gap before the signing, and the parties agree to a renegotiation clause.
- **Friction:** The bison-hide belting demand still arrives, but the treaty's tripwire never fires.
- **Rock:** The translator's own background, from a family that worked the Spanish frontier, makes the encoding gap visible.
- **Opening image:** A signing table where both sides sign different documents in their heads.
- **Drift:** The Comanche corridor survives in altered form. The Arkansas valley carries different goods.
- **Closing image:** A bison herd crossing the Arkansas River, with a new survey marker on the bank.

### Backup Seasons

Held in reserve for the back half of the series or a second run.

- **The Otrar Envoys (Khwarazm, 1218):** The Shah does not execute the Mongol trade envoys. The caravan route reroutes through a different valley. *Good for a mid-series reconfiguration of Central Asian power.*
- **The Yasa (Timur, 1370–1405):** Timur's successors build the legitimating middleware the Timurids never produced. *Good for showing the Qin-Han pattern from the inside.*
- **The Khwaday-Namag (Sasanian Persia, 602–628):** Khosrow II accepts a negotiated peace with Heraclius, and the mutual exhaustion that opened the Arab conquest never happens. *Alternate opener if Season 1 is cut.*

## Research note on Season 5 — the premise needs relocating

Checked 2026-10-08, because this is the seed season and the premise should be sound before anything is built on it. Two findings, one fatal to the stated break and one that is a gift.

**The lever is in the wrong place.** The East/West zone boundary was not set at Yalta. The European Advisory Commission approved a protocol on **12 September 1944** delineating three occupation zones, with the Soviet zone running roughly east of the Elbe. Yalta's modification to that scheme was the addition of a *French* zone, carved out of British and American territory. So "Roosevelt dies before Yalta" does not move the Elbe line — the line was drawn five months earlier, and Yalta inherited it. Nor did the armies halt at the Elbe *because* of Yalta; that was Eisenhower's own military decision in late March 1945.

If the line is supposed to move, the break has to sit in the EAC negotiations of 1944, not at Yalta. The alternative, and probably the better drama, is to move the break *later* — see below.

**History already ran the experiment, and the restoring force is documented.** American forces did push beyond the agreed boundary, in places by as much as 200 miles. They held that ground for two months. And in the first days of **July 1945** they withdrew to the agreed line, trading Thuringia and Saxony for the four-sector division of Berlin.

That is Rule 3 — visible break, slow return — already in the historical record rather than invented. It is also an unusually honest break by Rule 2's own test: remove the rock and you get the same outcome, and you get it within two months rather than a generation. Which raises the real dramatic problem. The restoring force here is so fast and so well-attested that the drift is not a season-long tendency, it is a summer. The season may need to be about the withdrawal decision itself — why a victorious army hands back 200 miles — rather than about the advance.

That version has the advantage of being the sharper story and of sitting on solid ground: the question becomes what the terrain and the logistics made unavoidable about July 1945, which is the book's argument exactly.

Sources: EAC Protocol on Zones of Occupation, 12 September 1944 (germanhistorydocs.org); Yalta's addition of the French zone; the July 1945 withdrawal from Thuringia and Saxony.
