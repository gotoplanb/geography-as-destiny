## The test, and the result

Chapter 9 argues that five mechanisms converged in a seven-year window to destroy the Comanche, one of them being that "a specific manufacturing innovation in the late 1860s converted bison hide from a modest commercial commodity into industrial drive belting." Dave's falsification condition: if the bison were already being killed off *before* the belting innovation existed, the causal chain inverts and the argument collapses into coincidence.

**The ordering holds, decisively.**

- **1870** — a German firm develops a new tanning process making bison leather pliable and commercially useful.
- **1871** — an English tannery works out how to turn a buffalo hide into machine belting, the leather driving looms and lathes across industry. Tanners in Europe and America adopt new methods for treating dry hides and the hide business booms.
- **1871** — intensive commercial hunting begins, concentrated in southern Nebraska and Kansas nearest the railroad.
- **1872–1874** — the mass slaughter. Roughly two thousand hide hunters in western Kansas, about fifteen animals each per day. Rail shipments across those three years total 1,378,359 hides.
- **1874–1878** — the southern herd is finished.

Innovation precedes and then immediately accompanies the killing. The causal direction the chapter asserts is the one the record supports.

## The correction: "late 1860s" is wrong, and wrong in the direction that weakens the chapter

The innovation dates to 1870–71, not the late 1860s. The printed text is off by two to four years, and the error is not neutral — it *loosens* a conjunction whose force comes from tightness.

What actually happened is better than what the chapter says: **the belting breakthrough and the start of intensive commercial hunting are the same year.** 1871 is both. That is not a mechanism arriving somewhere inside a seven-year window; it is a demand shock and a hunt beginning together, with the mass killing following within twelve months. Stated accurately, the passage gets stronger.

Appendix D line 393 brackets this as "1860s–1870s CE," which is defensible as a chronology entry but inherits the same looseness.

**Recommended for a future edition:** replace "in the late 1860s" with 1871, or with "in 1870–71," and consider making the same-year coincidence explicit, since it is the sharpest version of the argument.

## Why this card exists: the boundary on the magnitude rule

Dave's formulation, which is the useful general statement: **correlation tolerates loose dating, causation does not.** Co-occurrence inside a seven-year window establishes that things happened together. It does not establish that one enabled the other. For that you need the ordering, and the ordering has to be secure against the error bars.

So the magnitude rule in CLAUDE.md has a boundary. Order-of-magnitude reasoning — centuries versus millennia — is the right register for most of this book, and insisting on year-precision there is false rigour. But at a **conjunction** or a **falsification**, year-precision becomes load-bearing, because the entire claim is about sequence. Chapter 9's seven-year window is the book's most precision-hungry passage for exactly this reason: five mechanisms, and the argument depends on all five landing inside the same window in a specific order.

This also gives reference cards a sharper job than "be as specific as the sources allow." **Record the uncertainty, not just the date.** A card reading "1860s" and a card reading "1871" are different instruments, and only the second can support a conjunction claim. A later reader needs to know which one they are holding.
