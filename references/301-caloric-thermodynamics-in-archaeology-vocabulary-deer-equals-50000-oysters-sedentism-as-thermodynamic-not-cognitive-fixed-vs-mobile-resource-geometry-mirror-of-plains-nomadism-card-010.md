## Content

Cunliffe builds his subsistence argument through **caloric equivalencies** — grams of food per unit area, and direct trade rates between resource types. Dave's delight: Cunliffe states that **one deer is worth ~50,000 oysters**. That single line is the most vivid possible statement of caloric thermodynamics applied to prehistoric subsistence economics.

The recognition: **Cunliffe is doing exactly what the framework does, in the vocabulary of archaeology rather than thermodynamics.** Food density per area, caloric equivalencies between protein sources — this is energy accounting applied to human prehistory. The vocabulary differs; the underlying logic is identical. Cunliffe builds the empirical foundation one data point at a time (grams per area, deer versus oysters); the framework supplies the **mechanism that connects those data points into a predictive model** (the card 297 / card 276 pattern: the literature describes and measures; the framework explains why and predicts).

The new analytical payoff is the thermodynamic theory of the **sedentism transition**:

**Sedentism is thermodynamic, not cognitive.** The shift from mobile foraging to coastal sedentism is not primarily a cognitive advance ("people got clever enough to settle down"). It is a caloric-return calculation. A productive oyster bed is a **fixed, high-density, reliable** calorie source. When the caloric return from staying near that fixed resource exceeds the caloric return from following mobile prey (deer herds), the rational response is to **stay**. The mind doing the calculation is the same mind in both the mobile and the sedentary population; the terrain's resource geometry sets which answer is rational.

**Fixed-vs-mobile resource geometry determines settlement geometry.** The deer and the oyster bed differ on the axis that matters for settlement:
- **Deer herd** — mobile, self-transporting, dispersed, high calories per individual but must be *chased*. Rewards mobility. (This is the Plains-bison logic of card 010: follow the resource that moves itself.)
- **Oyster bed** — fixed, stationary, dense per area, reliable, labor-intensive per calorie but it *stays put*. Rewards sedentism: build a base, return to it, defend it.

The geometry of the resource is rectified into the geometry of the settlement. A landscape whose richest reliable calories are *fixed and dense* selects for sedentism; a landscape whose richest calories are *mobile and dispersed* selects for nomadism. Same uniformly distributed human capacity; opposite rational settlement strategy, set by the resource's spatial structure.

This makes card 301 the explicit **mirror of card 010**. Card 010: the Great Plains rationally did *not* settle, because the buffalo economy made mobility the optimum (the resource self-transports). Card 301: the productive coast rationally *did* settle, because the oyster bed made staying the optimum (the resource is fixed and dense). Two halves of one law: **resource geometry → settlement geometry**, with caloric return as the rectifier. The framework's probabilistic frame holds throughout (per card 010): geography does not determine "settle or not"; it shifts the probability distribution of which strategy is rational.

Cunliffe, working in archaeology's own idiom, arrives at the same accounting (grams/area, deer = 50,000 oysters) without the framework's vocabulary. The framework is not importing an alien physics onto archaeology; it is naming the mechanism the careful empirical work already implies. The vivid datum (deer = 50,000 oysters) is a keeper as the cleanest one-line statement of the lens.

**2. The framework supplies the mechanism that connects Cunliffe's data points.** This is the recurring division of labor (cards 276, 297): Cunliffe measures and describes — one caloric equivalency at a time — but does not connect the points into a predictive mechanism. The framework's contribution is the connecting model: caloric return + resource geometry → rational settlement strategy → the settlement pattern observed. The framework turns Cunliffe's empirical points into a curve.

**3. A thermodynamic theory of sedentism extends the apparatus into the settlement axis.** Cards 299 and 300 read the *subsistence-technology* response (cultivation vs. seafaring) selected by terrain differentials. Card 301 reads a different axis — *settlement pattern* (mobile vs. sedentary) — selected by resource *geometry* (fixed vs. mobile) via caloric return. Together: terrain differentials select the subsistence *technology*; resource geometry selects the settlement *form*. Both are the receiver-side mechanism (terrain directing uniformly distributed capacity), now operating on two distinct axes. This is genuine apparatus extension, not restatement.

**4. It is the same anti-cognitive, anti-great-man commitment at the origin of sedentism.** "Sedentism as thermodynamic, not cognitive" is the card 283/284 commitment (intelligence uniformly distributed; terrain directs the response) applied to settlement. The sedentary coastal population was not smarter than the mobile inland one; their coast's fixed dense resource made staying the caloric optimum. Settling down is not a rung on a cognitive ladder; it is a terrain-rational response to a fixed high-density resource.

## Calibration

1. **"Deer = 50,000 oysters" is an illustrative order-of-magnitude equivalency.** Reported from Dave's listening of Cunliffe Ch. 3; the exact ratio depends on deer size and oyster species/size (a red-deer carcass is on the order of 10⁵ kcal of usable meat; an oyster only ~5–15 kcal — so the figure is the right order of magnitude). The analytical point — caloric accounting as the right frame — does not rest on the precise number. Keep it as the vivid illustration it is, not as a load-bearing statistic.

2. **The oyster bed wins on fixity + density + reliability, not on labor-efficiency.** Oysters are *labor-intensive per calorie* (that is what the 50,000 figure encodes). The rationality of sedentism is not that oysters are cheap calories — it is that a fixed, dense, reliable resource you can *return to and defend* enables a sedentary base in a way mobile dispersed prey cannot. State the mechanism precisely: fixity + density + reliability enabling a sedentary base, not per-labor efficiency. Beta's framing is correct on this and should be preserved.

3. **Sedentism is multi-causal; thermodynamic return is the dominant terrain-set driver, not the sole cause.** Social, defensive, storage, demographic, and ritual factors also bear on sedentism. The framework reads caloric return + resource geometry as the dominant *terrain-set incentive* without claiming monocausality. Note the corroboration: pre-agricultural sedentary foragers (e.g., the Natufian, settling near rich fixed wild resources *before* farming) are exactly what the thermodynamic reading predicts — sedentism near a fixed dense resource, ahead of cultivation. Sedentism-before-agriculture supports the mechanism rather than complicating it.

4. **This is pre-horse / pre-sail deep-prehistory substrate (card 165 scope).** The framework reads the thermodynamic-settlement logic here but does not run its fast-dynamics apparatus on the Mesolithic. Card 301 is substrate/origins material, consistent with the framework's candid scope for this period.

5. **The fixed-resource → sedentism logic connects forward to the river-spine apparatus.** The river valley is the framework's canonical *fixed linear attractor* (card 047: the Nile traps population along the only water gradient; cards 070, 277: civilizations succeed along the energy gradient). The oyster bed is the same logic in miniature — a fixed dense resource organizing settlement around itself. Worth holding the continuity: fixed dense resource → sedentism → (at river scale) concentration → administrative complexity.
