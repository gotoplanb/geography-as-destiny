# Polynesian Relative-Direction System as Rogers Compatibility — Coordinate System Not Absolutely Better

**Source:** Beta seminar discussion, 2026-06-16, during Dave's listening of Christina Thompson, *Sea People: The Puzzle of Polynesia* (William Collins, 2019), Part 3
**Type:** connection
**Relevant to:** Diffusion lens (Rogers compatibility), Shannon receiver-side cluster, Chapter 4 (Silk Road / encoding cards), general thesis

## Content

The Polynesian relative-direction system encodes direction as positional relations to known features. *Ma uka* and *ma kai* — toward the mountains, toward the sea. The rising and setting points of named stars. The wind quarters with their seasonal names. The distance and bearing of known landfalls. From the inside this is not approximate. Within the known world it is functionally complete. The navigator does not need a coordinate. He needs to know, from where he is, the bearing and distance to where he is going. The relative system gives him that with no abstraction at all.

The European coordinate system — latitude, longitude, north as absolute — looks obviously superior from the perspective of a literate observer with a chronometer. But "obviously superior" is the receiver-side trap. The coordinate system is superior for navigation *beyond the known world* — for transoceanic voyaging into spaces not previously charted by anyone in the receiver's tradition. Within the known world it offers nothing the relative system doesn't already give, and it imposes heavy cognitive overhead: abstraction, instrumentation, calculation.

This is Rogers compatibility in its sharpest form. The compatibility variable in Rogers's adoption-curve apparatus is not "is this objectively better." It is "does this address a problem the receiver actually has." Polynesian navigators in 18th-century Polynesia did not have the problem the coordinate system solves. They had a working solution to the problem they actually faced. The coordinate system was not adopted because it offered nothing — *for that use case*. It was adopted, eventually, when Polynesian navigators were drawn into a different practice (long-distance commercial sailing, military service, geodetic survey) that did face the problem coordinates solve.

This generalizes. Many "obviously superior" innovations fail to diffuse not because the receiving culture is conservative or unsophisticated but because the innovation solves a problem the receivers don't have. Receiver-side compatibility is the gate. The framework's existing cards on papermaking diffusion (cards 030, 079) treat the gap as a channel problem — broken political channels between Han China and the Islamic world. The Polynesian case adds a complementary gate. Even when the channel is open, the innovation must fit a need the receiver has. Some innovations fail to diffuse not because they couldn't but because the receivers had no problem for them to solve.

## Why this matters

This refines the framework's diffusion lens in two ways.

1. **Rogers compatibility is not just cultural fit — it is use-case fit.** The book's existing diffusion cards lean on compatibility as cultural and institutional fit (Manichaeism's syncretic design; Buddhism's translatability into Tang Chinese institutions). The Polynesian case adds a sharper variable: the receiver must actually face the problem the innovation solves. This is closer to Christensen's jobs-to-be-done than to Rogers's original formulation, and it tightens the framework's prediction. Innovations that fail to diffuse despite a working channel are often failing the use-case test, not the cultural test.

2. **Shannon-style encoding optimization is receiver-specific.** The relative-direction system is optimally encoded for one receiver: the navigator inside Polynesia. The coordinate system is optimally encoded for a different receiver: the navigator anywhere on Earth, including in spaces no one in his tradition has been. "Better encoding" is meaningless without specifying the receiver. The framework already says this for written sources; the navigation case extends it to embodied skill-sets and shows that the same logic governs which technologies get adopted across cultural boundaries.

This also strengthens the framework's anti-Whig disposition. The European observers who concluded the Polynesian system was primitive because it lacked coordinates were reading a sophisticated, use-case-optimal encoding as a defective version of their own. The receiver-side discipline rejects this reading. The Polynesians had the better tool for their problem. Europeans had the better tool for theirs. Geography selected the problem each had to solve. The encoding optimized for that.
