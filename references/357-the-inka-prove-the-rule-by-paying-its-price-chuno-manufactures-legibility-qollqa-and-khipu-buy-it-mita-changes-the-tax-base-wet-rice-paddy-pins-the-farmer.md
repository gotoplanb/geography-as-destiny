# The Inka Prove the Rule by Paying Its Price — Four State Responses to an Illegible Staple / Chuño Manufactures Legibility, Qollqa and Khipu Buy It, Mit'a Changes the Tax Base / Wet Rice Pins the Farmer as Well as the Crop

**Source:** Dave, Beta seminar, 2026-11-03, reading Scott, *Against the Grain*. Dave reports Scott arguing that tubers are the worst case for a state, that rice is the best, and that **for a state to exist it must narrow the available food base** to grain. Beta supplied the wet-rice immobility point and raised the Inka as a possible counterexample. Alpha verified the Andean material and found it sharpens rather than weakens the claim.
**Type:** connection / framework extension
**Relevant to:** card 344 (cost to observe × cost to seize), card 356 (legibility is generative), card 345 (exit cost), card 350 (mandala states, Zomia), Chapter 4 (River Spine)

## Scott's claim as reported, and why rice tops the list

A diverse subsistence base — tubers, nuts, fish, game, gardens — is a hedge for the household and a nightmare for the assessor. So state formation involves a quiet act of simplification: **the landscape is made readable by shrinking what grows on it.** That is card 356's generative legibility stated as a precondition rather than a consequence, and it is a stronger claim than card 344 currently makes.

**Beta's wet-rice point is the strongest form of the immobility term.** A paddy is fixed capital — levelled, bunded, tied to water control, labour-expensive to build. A farmer cannot pick up a paddy and walk into the forest. So wet rice does not only make the *crop* visible and immovable; it pins the *population* to the land that grows it. Both halves of 344's variable are maximized at once, which is why it sits at the top of the list. This also explains the Southeast Asian pattern in card 350: mandala states cluster where wet rice is possible, and the swidden and tuber cultivators of the hills stay out of reach — the region Scott later names Zomia.

## The Inka: not a refutation, a price tag

Beta raised the Andes as a counterexample — a large state on potatoes and maize rather than on a Eurasian grain. It holds as a counterexample to any *hard* rule, and verification shows something more useful: the Inka did not find a way around the legibility problem. **They paid for legibility four separate times.**

- **Transform the crop.** *Chuño* — potatoes freeze-dried by repeated exposure to night frost and day sun — converts a perishable, heavy, in-ground tuber into something light, durable and countable that keeps for years. This is not storing a tuber; it is **manufacturing a legible commodity out of an illegible one.** The inverse of Scott's mechanism: rather than selecting the legible crop, make the crop legible.
- **Build the observation infrastructure.** *Qollqa* state storehouses, sited on hillsides for cool air and engineered with drainage canals, gravel floors and floor-and-roof ventilation — ordinary goods keeping about two years, freeze-dried foodstuffs up to four.
- **Build the accounting system.** *Khipu*, in the absence of writing, tracking how much maize and how many potatoes and tunics sat in each storehouse, what the latest harvest added, and what was dispatched to a moving army.
- **Change what is taxed.** *Mit'a* levied **labour rather than produce** — households owed periods of work for agriculture, construction, military service and infrastructure, with the state feeding them from the storehouses in return. If the harvest is hard to see, tax attendance instead, which is not.

So the honest reading is that **grain states get legibility free from the crop, and the Inka had to buy it** — with a processing technology, a storage architecture, a record-keeping system and a reconstructed tax base. That is a plausible explanation for the famous administrative density of the Inka state: it is the bill for statehood without a legible staple.

That converts Scott's claim from a necessary condition into a cost argument, which is where this project's apparatus wants it anyway. **Grain is the cheapest path to a state, not the only one.** Dave's cost-to-observe framing predicts exactly this: where observation is expensive, a state either pays for it or does not form.

## The taxonomy this yields

Four responses available to a state facing an illegible food base, and the Inka ran three of them simultaneously:

1. **Narrow the base** to an already-legible crop — Scott's grain states.
2. **Process the staple** into a legible commodity — chuño.
3. **Buy observation** with infrastructure — qollqa, khipu.
4. **Move the tax base** off produce entirely — mit'a on labour.

Testable: the prediction is that states on illegible staples should show systematically heavier administrative apparatus per unit of extraction than grain states, because they are purchasing what grain supplies for nothing.
