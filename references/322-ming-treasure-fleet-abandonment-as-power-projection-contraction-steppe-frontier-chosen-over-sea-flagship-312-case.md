## The thesis (Dave's, confirmed) — withdrawal as contraction, not culture
The standard story credits the fleet's end to **Confucian scholar-officials defeating the eunuch faction** (Zheng He was a eunuch) and a cultural preference for an agrarian, land-based order. That factional fight is real — but it is the **proximate mechanism, not the cause**. The framework's reading, which the fiscal record supports: the treasure fleet was **discretionary power projection**, and it was cut because the Ming's **power-projection budget was being consumed by an existential two-front problem** — the steppe (Mongol/Oirat) frontier in the north. Facing a two-front choice between *discretionary maritime prestige* and *existential continental defense*, the empire did the structurally forced thing: it **abandoned the sea to hold the land.** The Confucian-agrarian faction won *because its program was the affordable one under contraction* — the resource constraint selected the winner (this is card 218's expansion→contraction phase-shift choosing the consolidation faction, exactly as Han Emperor Wu's expansion gave way to extraction/consolidation).

**Fiscal confirmation:** each voyage cost ~**2–3 million taels of silver — over 10% of state revenue**; scholarship attributes the termination primarily to the **fiscal strain of the Mongol wars plus Great Wall reconstruction pulling funds north.** Discretionary maritime projection is precisely what gets cut first when the margin tightens.

## Why this is the flagship case for card 312
Card 312's claim: **power projection peaks and turns down *first*; physical presence (population, wealth, monuments) lags and can still be rising** — so "decline" means the *projection* curve is past peak, not that presence is shrinking. The Ming is the cleanest possible illustration:
- At the moment the fleet is scrapped, Ming **presence** is enormous and *still rising* — the largest economy on earth, a vast population, a new capital at Beijing, the treasury and cities impressive. Nothing "looks like decline."
- But Ming **projection** has already turned: the reach that sent 27,000 men to the Swahili coast is being pulled home to defend the northern wall line. The projection curve peaked under Yongle and turned down; the presence curve kept climbing.
- **The treasure fleet is the projection curve made visible** — the single most legible act of long-range projection any pre-modern state performed — and its cancellation is the projection curve turning down *while the presence curve was still going up*. That is card 312's entire thesis in one event. Promote the Ming as 312's flagship case.

## The sequence (the precision that matters — don't let Tumu "cause" the end)
Keep the chronology exact, because the causal story depends on it:
1. **Yongle Emperor (r. 1402–1424): peak dual projection.** Sponsors the voyages (1405–), campaigns *personally* five times against the Mongols, and **moves the capital Nanjing → Beijing** (completed 1421) — binding the dynasty's fate to the northern frontier ("the emperor guards the gate"). Projection is at maximum on *both* axes at once — and the continental campaigns are already the expensive, existential ones.
2. **Voyages end 1433** (7th voyage, under Xuande). The **discretionary maritime axis is cut first** as the fiscal margin tightens — projection withdrawing from the sea while the northern commitment holds.
3. **Tumu Crisis, 1449 — the lock-in, not the cause.** The Oirats under Esen **capture the Zhengtong Emperor** in the field. This is *after* the voyages already ended, so it did not end them — it **cements and vindicates** the continental-defensive turn already underway, killing any residual appetite for discretionary projection.
4. **Ming Great Wall as the fortification-signature.** The monumental brick Great Wall we picture today is largely a **Ming** construction (15th–16th c.) — the material embodiment of the continental turn, and a textbook instance of **card 190**: you build walls when you *cannot project power far enough to stop the threat at distance.* The Wall is behavioral evidence that Ming projection had turned down; it is the Chinese analog of Old Europe's fortifications (190–191), and it doubles as the Silk Road chokepoint control surface (cards 29/226).

## The masked-risk twist (ties 312 → 321/250)
Card 312's sharpest point is that **the shock lands in the gap** between the turned-down projection curve and the still-high presence. The Ming case has a poignant long-range version: the reallocation was **locally rational** — the steppe was the *real, present* threat, and Tumu proved it within 16 years. But projection was withdrawn from the **maritime** axis, and it was the maritime axis that would become decisive **four centuries later**, when European sea power arrived (Portuguese from the 1510s) and, ultimately, delivered the **Opium Wars** (cards 250/321). The Ming defended the frontier that was visibly threatening and abandoned the frontier that would prove fatal — not a blunder (the maritime cost was centuries-deferred and unforeseeable), but a clean illustration of **projection-withdrawal on the axis where the masked risk was quietly maturing.** And note the through-line to card 321: China stayed the world's bullion *sink* but stopped *projecting* to control the maritime route it had once dominated — leaving that route for others to seize.

## Why this matters
- **Converts owned apparatus into a documented case.** The framework had 312/218/190/237/233 but no Ming case; this makes the Ming the flagship worked example of power-projection contraction, which is more persuasive than the abstract statement.
- **Anti-great-man dividend.** Reframes the "Confucians killed the fleet" story: the faction fight is proximate; the two-front power-projection budget is why *that* faction won. Individuals/factions are real but play out a structurally set distribution (the framework's core discipline).
- **Links the maritime and continental threads** (321 ↔ 312 ↔ 250): the same withdrawal that ended China's maritime projection is the upstream of the European maritime ascendancy and the eventual Opium-Wars reversal.

## Calibration / open items
1. **Sequence discipline: 1433 voyages-end precedes 1449 Tumu.** Tumu *locks in* the turn; it does not cause the fleet's end. Any prose must preserve this or the causal story breaks.
2. **The factional/ideological cause is real, not to be erased.** Confucian-vs-eunuch politics and the sea-ban (*hai jin*) are genuine proximate mechanisms; the framework claim is that the *structural* two-front constraint selected the outcome, not that ideology was irrelevant. Both/and.
3. **Don't moralize the "wrong frontier" twist.** Defending the north was locally correct (Tumu proved the threat). The maritime-abandonment cost was centuries-deferred and not foreseeable; present it as 312's masked-risk gap maturing over the long run, not as Ming shortsightedness.
4. **Verify load-bearing specifics before prose.** Per-voyage cost (~2–3M taels / >10% revenue), the 1433/1449 dates, the capital-move date (1421), and "the brick Great Wall is largely Ming" are all well-attested but worth a citation pass; Dave verifies.
5. **This is a Ming *case* card; the general apparatus stays in 312/218/190.** Cross-reference, don't re-derive the mechanism.
