# Friction Collapse as Energy Capture — Every Physical-Mobility Collapse Harnesses a New Energy Source (Grass, Wind, Coal) / The Herd Is Outsourced Digestion That Walks / Fire Is the Same Capture Without the Movement

**Source:** Dave, Beta seminar, 2026-11-04, extending card 360 (fire) while reading Scott, *Against the Grain*, Ch. 1. Dave's point: the framework begins with the horse, and the horse's friction collapse is not really about movement — it is a *mobile energy generator* that grazes grass humans cannot eat, can find forage under light snow, and lets people move calories far more densely than carrying root vegetables by hand. Beta conceded that its own movement/energy split (card 360) was too clean, called the horse "outsourced digestion on legs," and offered a unifying line: fire, the horse and the plow crops all convert inaccessible energy into usable energy, and differ in how much movement comes with it.
**Type:** framework refinement — re-grounds one of Chapter 1's three friction-collapse categories; candidate for Charlie
**Relevant to:** Chapter 1 (the three categories of friction collapse and the "who does the terrain serve" test), Bronze Age substrate interlude, cards 360 (fire), 204 (two friction collapses), 199/268 (wagon; previously unuseful land), 188 (cold killed the cattle; snow pawing), 276 (thermodynamics as the deepest foundation), 301 (caloric thermodynamics), 344/349 (the cost thread)

## The claim, sharpened

Chapter 1 sorts friction collapses into three categories: physical mobility, information persistence, and coordination across difference. Physical mobility is defined as "the cost of moving across terrain," and the members listed are horse domestication, oceanic sail, steam and rail.

Dave's point, generalized: **every member of that category is the capture of a new energy source that was already present in the landscape but inaccessible to people.**

| Collapse | Energy captured | Who could use it before |
|---|---|---|
| Grazing herd (ruminants, then the horse) | Grassland cellulose | Nobody — humans cannot digest it |
| Oceanic sail | Wind over open water | Only coasting craft, by day |
| Steam and rail | Coal: fossil sunlight | Almost nobody, at scale |
| *Fire / cooking (card 360)* | *Chemical energy locked in raw tissue and starch* | *Only the animals with the guts for it* |

The movement comes *after* the energy capture: once the energy is accessible, it can be spent on traction and transport. Fire is the limiting case — the same capture with almost no movement attached. That is why it felt like it sat outside the framework. It doesn't. It is the zero-movement end of the same axis.

**This explains Chapter 1's test rather than replacing it.** The test is whether a technology changes *who the terrain serves*. The energy reading says *why* some technologies pass and others don't:
- **A collapse passes when it makes terrain's stored energy usable by a different population.** The steppe's grass was a barrier to farmers and a living to herders. Coal turned the same steppe into the state's ground, because rail ran on energy the nomads could not field.
- **"A faster horse" fails because it captures no new energy.** It spends the same grass better. That is an improvement within a regime, which is exactly how Chapter 1 classifies it.

## Two corrections the card has to carry

**1. The first collapse ran on ruminants, not horses — so Dave's point applies to the Yamnaya more strongly than he stated it.** The project redated the horse in May (cards 164, 204): the modern domestic horse lineage (DOM2) emerges in the lower Don-Volga around 2200 BCE (Librado et al. 2021, *Nature*), and Botai belongs to the Przewalski lineage. Beta raised this as a correction, but it is already the project's position, and the Bronze Age interlude prose already uses the two-collapse structure.

The consequence Beta missed: in card 204's structure, the *first* collapse (~3300 BCE) is the **wagon plus cattle and sheep**. So the original energy-on-legs was the ruminant herd, with the wagon carrying water and household between pastures. Ruminants are the better cellulose converters. That makes Dave's "mobile energy generator" claim *more* central to the framework's anchor than "the horse" implies: the first friction collapse was an energy capture from the start.

The horse's distinct contribution is speed and reach at ~2200 BCE (the DOM2 chariot), plus the winter advantage:
- **The snow-pawing detail is already in card 188.** Horses dig through snow to reach grass where cattle and sheep cannot.
- **Timing caveat:** card 188 ties it to the ~4200 BCE cold event, before DOM2. So it describes pre-DOM2 horse herding, not the horse that spread.

**2. Yamnaya drank ruminant milk; horse milk and blood belong to other peoples.** Beta's caveat is right, and it can be made specific:
- **Yamnaya:** dental-calculus proteins point to milk from cattle, sheep and goats (Wilkin et al. 2021, *Nature*, on Yamnaya dairying).
- **Mare's milk:** better attested at Botai (lipid residues; Outram et al. 2009, *Science*) and in much later steppe pastoralism, including the Mongolian sequence (Wilkin et al. 2020, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*).
- **Blood-drinking:** an ethnographic and historical steppe practice, not a documented Yamnaya one.

**Verify both Wilkin citations before relying on them; they are from memory.**

## Where the energy reading stops

- **It re-grounds the physical-mobility category only.** Writing, printing, the telegraph and lingua francas are not energy captures. They lower the cost of information and of coordination. The claim is not that every friction collapse is about energy. It is that the physical ones are, and that this is what Chapter 1's "physical mobility" was measuring indirectly.
- **"Thermodynamics" stays literal here.** This is energy accounting in calories and joules, which card 276 named as the framework's deepest layer. That keeps it testable: for each candidate collapse, name the energy source and who could use it before. If a proposed physical collapse captures no new source, the framework predicts it will turn out to be an improvement, not a collapse.
- **The "plow crops" in Beta's line need care.** Grain agriculture concentrates solar energy onto a field rather than capturing a new source, and it immobilizes people rather than moving them. It fits the energy axis better than the movement axis, which is exactly Beta's point. But it should be filed as an intensification, not called a friction collapse, unless it passes the who-does-the-terrain-serve test. Arguably it does (farmland serves the state; card 344), but that needs its own argument.

## Why this matters

1. **It closes the gap between the framework's two vocabularies.** The book already speaks of thermodynamics (gradients, pressure, card 276) and of friction (cost of crossing terrain). This card says they are the same account: friction falls when a new energy source becomes available to spend against it.
2. **It brings fire, cooking and the herd inside the framework without stretching it.** Fire is no longer outside the scope; it sits at the zero-movement end of the energy-capture axis, at a timescale that makes it substrate (card 360).
3. **It is structural, so it goes to Charlie before prose.** Re-grounding a published category is a structural decision. If it holds, Chapter 1's "physical mobility" paragraph gains one clause: *each of these captured an energy source already in the landscape.* By the project's release rules that would be a minor release, not a patch.

## Status

Card 360 (fire) is promoted from a standalone stub to the limiting case of this card.
