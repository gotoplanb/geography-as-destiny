# Papua New Guinea — Appropriability, Not Storability, Is the Binding Constraint / Surplus, Prestige Goods and Relay Trade All Present, States Absent / Scott's Grain-vs-Tuber Mechanism Isolates the Variable the Corridor Chapters Cannot

**Source:** Beta seminar discussion, 2026-09-16, following Dave's ElevenReader listening of "Why I Can't Stop Thinking About Papua" (notnottalmud.substack.com). Follow-up research 2026-09-16 — see Verification below. The essay itself is popular press, cites no academic source for its central claim, and is wrong on at least one point of fact.
**Type:** connection / natural experiment / framework sharpening
**Relevant to:** Chapter 1 (the distribution; convergence), Chapter 4 (River Spine — surplus and class structure), Chapter 6 (Silk Road as relay network — this is the relay without the state), the receiver-side agriculture cluster (299, 301), the isolation cluster (279)

## The claim as it arrived, and why it does not survive contact

The essay's chain: taro and banana rot within weeks, therefore no stored surplus, therefore no freed labour, therefore no prestige goods, therefore no trade, therefore a thousand small warring polities. Dave's initial read was that this is a confirmation case that does not extend the framework.

Research does not support the chain as stated. Four of its links fail:

- **Agriculture is deep and independent.** Kuk Swamp shows plant exploitation and some cultivation at 10,220–9,910 cal BP, mounded cultivation by 6,950–6,440 cal BP, ditched cultivation by 4,350–3,980 cal BP. Bananas were intensively cultivated by ~7,000 cal BP. New Guinea is a genuine independent centre of agricultural origin (Denham et al., *Science*, 2003).
- **Surplus existed.** It was held in pigs. Big-men "amass resources over years" and redistribute them publicly in the Melpa *moka* and Enga *tee*. The post-Columbian sweet potato (~300 years ago) drove the "Ipomoean revolution," expanding both population and pig herds specifically as fodder for ceremonial exchange.
- **Prestige goods existed.** Pearlshells and polished stone axes, both circulating as valuables.
- **Long-distance trade existed.** Polished axes from a limited number of quarry areas were distributed across large distances *through a series of intermediary groups*, 2,500–1,500 BP; five trade routes are identified among lowland communities, with the Kikori record running back ~4,000 years; shell valuables moved inland from the coast, and Austronesian coastal contact around 3,000 BP registers at Kuk.

The essay also states New Guinea had "no domesticated animals." Pigs are the centre of the entire highland exchange economy. That error is load-bearing for its argument.

## What actually does the work: Scott's grain-vs-tuber mechanism

James C. Scott, *Against the Grain* (2017), supplies the rigorous version, and it **inverts** the essay's reasoning while reaching a similar endpoint. Cereal grains are taxable because they are "visible, divisible, assessable, storable, transportable, and 'rationable'" — above all, they ripen simultaneously and above ground, so a state can see the harvest and take a share of it. Roots and tubers are, in Scott's phrase, *nearly appropriation-proof*: they can be safely left in the ground for up to two years and dug piecemeal as needed.

So the constraint is not that tubers cannot be stored. They store better than grain — in the ground, indefinitely, for free. The constraint is that they cannot be **appropriated**. There is no harvest moment, no visible pile, nothing to assess or confiscate. Scott's own distinction is between "state" crops and "state-evading" crops.

This relocates the mechanism from the *producer* side to the *extraction* side, which is a different claim than the one the essay makes and a better fit for the evidence.

## Why this matters — stronger than a confirmation case

Dave's instinct was that this adds nothing at the macro level. The research suggests otherwise, and in the same way the Tecumseh correction did: the accurate version is sharper than the plausible one.

New Guinea is a natural experiment that isolates a variable the corridor chapters cannot. It had, simultaneously:

- deep independent agriculture
- accumulated surplus (in livestock)
- craft specialisation and prestige goods
- **a long-distance relay trade network moving valuables through intermediary groups** — structurally the same form Chapter 6 identifies in the Silk Road: a relay, not a road

and it produced no states. Every ingredient the book associates with corridor development was present except one, and the outcome did not follow.

That isolates appropriability as the operative variable. Trade alone does not build states. Surplus alone does not build states. A staple someone can count and take does. This is the cleanest available test of that proposition, because everything else is held constant by the island.

It also cuts hard for the anti-great-man and anti-eugenic commitments (283, 284, 260): nothing here turns on the capability of the people involved, who independently invented agriculture and ran continent-scale exchange networks. The binding constraint is what the available cultigens will do when a tax collector arrives.

Relation to the isolation cluster: 279 has terrain producing divergence without an ocean (KhoeSan; the Congo chimp–bonobo split). New Guinea stacks an ocean *and* highland topography — the maximum-intensity version. Relation to 299 and 301: those establish food *density* and fixed-vs-mobile resource geometry as drivers of settlement. Appropriability is a third, independent axis. Density, mobility, appropriability.
