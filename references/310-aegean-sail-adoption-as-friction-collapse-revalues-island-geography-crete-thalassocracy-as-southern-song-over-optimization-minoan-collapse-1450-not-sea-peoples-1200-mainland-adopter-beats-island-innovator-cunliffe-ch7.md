## Content

**The friction collapse.** Deep-hulled, sail-driven plank vessels had been working the Nile–Levant (eastern Mediterranean) routes for centuries before the Aegean adopted them at scale. When they arrive in the Aegean they **displace the paddled canoe/longboat** — very likely the same longboat tradition that had carried the earlier northern-Mediterranean maritime Neolithic (cards 300, 305). This is a textbook friction collapse: the cost of *open-water crossing* falls sharply, and — crucially — **it does not lower all friction uniformly; it revalues geography.** This is the Rogers lens too: the Aegean is a *late adopter* of a technology already mature elsewhere, and the adoption timing is set by terrain/sea-geometry (card 305's threshold-relative-to-baseline point), not by any deficit of ingenuity.

**What the revaluation does to power centers.** Under canoe/coastal-hugging trade, value accrues to *continuous coastline* — mainland ports with good harbors and unbroken shore access (the Anatolian and mainland centers). Once vessels can cross open water directly, **central position beats continuous access**, and a *defensible island* flips from liability to prime asset. Crete — central in the Aegean, ringed by sea — goes from isolating to optimal. Power reorients: Anatolia/mainland ports → island Crete as the **hub of a hub-and-spoke network**. Dave's read is exactly the friction-collapse mechanism: *the same terrain, revalued by a transport-cost change, reorganizes where power can sit* (cf. cards 013, 042, 300).

**The over-optimization trap (Beta's synthesis, kept).** An island hub-and-spoke thalassocracy is *brilliantly* optimized for the new trade conditions and *structurally fragile* in exactly the framework's sense: **no interior heartland, no overland retreat, and a system that requires continuous naval supremacy across the whole network simultaneously to function.** This is the **Southern-Song commercial over-optimization pattern** (cards 235, 237, 282): optimize hard for the conditions that exist, lose the redundancy that survives conditions changing. It is also the **island "painted-into-a-corner" geometry** of card 251 (Taiwan / first-island-chain): the island position that is efficient in calm becomes a trap under pressure.

**The correction — two collapses, not one (this is the load-bearing part).** Dave's and Beta's synthesis blurred the **Minoan collapse into the Bronze Age Collapse / Sea Peoples**. They are two distinct events ~250 years apart, and Cunliffe's own chronology keeps them separate:

- **Minoan collapse, ~1450 BCE.** The Thera/Santorini eruption (radiocarbon ~1600 BCE; traditional ~1500 BCE) damaged but did *not* end Minoan civilization — the palaces ran on for ~150 years after it. The decisive event is the **final palace destructions ~1450 BCE and the Mycenaean (mainland Greek) takeover of Knossos**, marked cleanly in the script record: **Linear A (Minoan) is replaced by Linear B (Mycenaean Greek).** The Minoans are *absorbed/eclipsed by a mainland power*.
- **Bronze Age Collapse, ~1200–1150 BCE.** The systemic failure — Mycenaean palaces, the Hittite empire, Ugarit and the Levantine city-states — with the "Sea Peoples" in the Egyptian record (Ramesses III, ~1177 BCE). This takes down the **Mycenaeans**, who had *inherited* the Aegean maritime sphere from the Minoans.

So **the Minoans did not fall to the Sea Peoples.** The Sea Peoples destroyed the power that had *replaced* the Minoans two and a half centuries earlier.

## Why this matters

**1. The correction strengthens the framework — it does not weaken it (the Tecumseh move).** Collapsing both events into "the Minoans burned out and fell with the Sea Peoples" is the *adequate-looking hallucination* that CLAUDE.md warns about. The corrected chronology is dramatically stronger because it yields **two clean, sequential over-optimization collapses** of the *same* type — and a precise mechanism for the first that the blurred version hides:

- **The mainland adopter beats the island innovator — and the framework predicts exactly this.** Crete *innovates* the island-hub optimization; Mycenae *adopts the same maritime technology* but sits on a mainland with an **interior heartland to fall back on**. When shock comes (Thera aftermath, disrupted networks, competition), the island-optimized system with no redundancy loses to the mainland adopter that kept its buffer. This is the framework's own "efficient-but-fragile vs. graceful-failure" thesis (card 282) *and* a diffusion story (Rogers: the innovator rarely captures the value of the innovation; the better-buffered adopter does). The blurred "it all fell to the Sea Peoples" version **erases this mechanism entirely.**
- **Then the pattern repeats one tier up.** Mycenae, having absorbed the maritime-palatial optimization, becomes the next over-optimized node and collapses in the systemic ~1200 event. Over-optimization → fragility fires *twice*, at two scales, in sequence. That is a far better demonstration of the principle than a single muddled burnout.

**2. Rein in "controlled the whole Mediterranean."** The Minoan "thalassocracy" is an *Aegean* phenomenon with eastern-Mediterranean trade/cultural contacts (Egypt, the Levant, the Cyclades, coastal Anatolia) — not control of the whole Mediterranean. The term *thalassocracy* is itself a later (Thucydidean) construct and its degree of political "control" vs. trade/cultural influence is genuinely debated. Keep the claim to "dominant Aegean maritime hub with far-flung eastern-Med contacts" — the argument does not need the overreach, and the overreach is a "color is a smell" flag (card 308 discipline applied to our own enthusiasm).

**3. All three lenses fire cleanly on the run-up, which is why it belongs in Chapter 1's toolkit demonstration.** *Geographic determinism*: sea geometry makes a central island the optimal node once crossing is cheap. *Friction collapse*: the sail revalues that geometry rather than lowering all friction evenly. *Diffusion*: the Aegean is a late Rogers adopter of a mature technology, and the value flows to the buffered adopter, not the innovator.
