**Type:** connection / framework consolidation

## The move

Card 344 establishes appropriability from the *state's* side: grain can be seen, counted and taken; tubers cannot. Dave's addition is the same variable from the *subject's* side. If the staple is appropriable and the terrain has no exit, participation is not a choice. You are in the system or you are nothing. If exit is cheap — another island, another valley, another range — then any polity that presses too hard simply loses its population.

Two independent terms, then, and terrain sets both:

1. **Appropriability** — can the surplus be assessed and taken?
2. **Exit cost** — can the population leave?

A state needs both to go its way. High appropriability with cheap exit gives you a tax base that walks away. Low appropriability with expensive exit gives you a captive population with nothing to confiscate. Durable states need a legible harvest *and* a population that cannot practically run.

## Scott wrote both halves

This is not a stray analogy. The same author wrote the companion volume. *Against the Grain* (2017) is the appropriability side. *The Art of Not Being Governed: An Anarchist History of Upland Southeast Asia* (2009) is the exit side: Zomia, a trans-regional upland space of 80–100 million people functioning as a "zone of refuge" from state demands for slaves, taxes, corvée labour and military service. The adaptations Scott catalogues are exit made durable — dispersed and mobile settlement, oral rather than written tradition, and deliberately cultivated **"escape crops"** chosen because they are hard for a state to map, administer and appropriate.

Note what Zomia's refugee populations look like from the outside: "minority peoples of truly bewildering ethnic and linguistic variety." That is the same thousand-tribes, thousand-languages outcome as New Guinea, produced by the same mechanism on different terrain — uplands doing the work an archipelago does. The linguistic diversity is not a curiosity of either place. It is the signature of a region where exit is cheap.

The formal vocabulary is Hirschman, *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970): where exit is cheap, voice is weak and loyalty is unnecessary. Coercive institutions cannot consolidate against an easy exit.

## The book already runs this argument — in Chapter 9

This is the important finding, and it answers the question card 344 left open about whether appropriability is a genuine fourth axis.

Chapter 9, on the destruction of the bison: *"The American administrative frustration with the Comanche was never about resource management capacity. It was about sedentarization."* And: *"Continued mobile existence meant no conversion to sedentary agriculture. No conversion meant the land could not be incorporated into the Anglo-American agricultural political economy."*

That is precisely the appropriability mechanism, stated in full and never named. A mobile pastoral economy running on a migratory resource is un-appropriable in Scott's exact sense — no fixed fields, no simultaneous harvest, no assessable pile. The bison campaign was not incidental to conversion; it was the instrument of it. Destroy the un-appropriable resource base and the population must adopt the appropriable one. Sheridan and Sherman understood the point and the chapter says so.

So appropriability is not a new axis to bolt onto the three lenses. It is already load-bearing in the flagship chapter. What card 344 and this one supply is the *name*, the comparative literature, and the observation that the Comanche case and the New Guinea case are the same argument seen from opposite ends — one where the state successfully forced appropriability, one where it never arrived to try.

Card 010 (Plains nomadism as rational optimization) gains the same reading: mobility is not only caloric optimization, it is a low-exit-cost posture.

## Evidence strength

- Zomia, escape crops, zone-of-refuge: well established, Scott 2009. Confident.
- Hirschman as the formal frame: standard, and the application here is straightforward.
- **Dave's Indonesia extension is NOT verified.** Scott's Zomia is *upland mainland* Southeast Asia. Whether the archipelago functions as a maritime equivalent — cheap exit by boat producing the same fragmentation — is a reasonable hypothesis and Dave hedged it himself ("not a perfect analogy"). It needs its own source before use. The obvious places to look are the literature on *orang laut* sea nomads, and on the mandala / negara model of Southeast Asian states, whose defining feature is weak territorial control and authority that decays with distance — which is what a cheap-exit environment should produce.
