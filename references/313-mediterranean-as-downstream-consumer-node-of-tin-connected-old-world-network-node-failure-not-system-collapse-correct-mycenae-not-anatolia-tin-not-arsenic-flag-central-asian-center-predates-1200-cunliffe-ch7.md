## The strong core to keep

Dave's load-bearing insight is correct and worth stating cleanly: **the Late Bronze Age Mediterranean was a downstream *consumer* node of an Old-World metals network, not the network's engine.** Tin was the **bottleneck / connective tissue** — copper was widely available, but tin (needed at ~10% for tin-bronze) was rare and had to be hauled from a few distant sources (Central Asian Zeravshan deposits, Afghan Badakhshan-adjacent sources, and European/Cornish tin from the west). So:

- **The ~1200 BCE collapse is a *node failure in a network*, not the collapse of Bronze Age civilization as such.** The over-optimized eastern-Mediterranean palatial consumer node shattered (cards 310/311); the tin-supplying interior and other nodes did not. This is exactly the framework's localized-fragility reading — the collapse hits the tightly-coupled downstream consumer, not the resource headwaters. Beta's affirmation of this is right, and it is the part to build on.
- The Med really was a *distant-tin-dependent* consumer node — but **which** distant source it drew on by ~1200 BCE is the live question, and current research points **west, not to Central Asia** (see Research Addendum below; the earlier draft of this bullet over-credited the Uluburun→Central Asia link, which is contested and probably minority). The node-failure logic survives regardless of *where* the tin came from; the specific Central-Asia→Med sourcing does not.

## Two corrections (fix in-card, per "verify, don't punt")

1. **The Mycenaeans were mainland Greek, not Anatolian.** Centered at Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos. **Anatolia in this period was the Hittite Empire** — a first-rank Bronze Age power that *also* fell in the ~1200 collapse. The nuance that makes Dave's "Mycenaeans in Anatolia" *partly* recoverable: the Mycenaeans held a **trading foothold on the west Anatolian coast (Miletus / Hittite "Millawanda")**, and **"Ahhiyawa"** in Hittite texts is widely read as the Mycenaean Greeks; the **Trojan War tradition sits exactly on this Mycenaean–Hittite contact seam.** So: Mycenaean *contact with and a toehold in* western Anatolia — yes; Mycenaean *base in* Anatolia — no. Anatolia belongs to the Hittites.

2. **The Central Asian bottleneck resource is *tin*, not "the right arsenic content."** Dave is fusing two different metallurgical traditions (card 181 already draws this line):
   - **Arsenical bronze** (copper + arsenic-rich ore) is the *earlier, alternative* technology (e.g., Maykop, ~3700–3500 BCE) — it needs no tin.
   - **Tin-bronze** (copper + tin) is the *later, dominant Late Bronze Age* alloy and is what creates the *long-distance* dependency, because tin is geographically rare.
   The "big mine in Central Asia" is a **tin** story: the **Zeravshan deposits (Karnab, Mushiston)** and the older **Sarazm** trading platform (~3500 BCE), organized through the **BMAC** oasis system (card 206). Arsenic is a red herring for the long-distance network; tin is the connective tissue.

## The flag — the chronology is inverted (→ GitHub issue)

Dave's framing: "as Mediterranean power waned, we are *centuries into* a huge industrial center being created in Central Asia that *will push west into Persia and east into China*." **The timing runs the other way.** From the framework's own Bronze Age cards:

- **The BMAC/Zeravshan "industrial center" peaked ~2200–1700 BCE** (card 206) — it was already *declining* ~500 years **before** the ~1200 BCE Mediterranean collapse, not rising as the Med fell.
- **The eastward metallurgical push predates 1200 too:** the **Seima-Turbino** phenomenon (~2000 BCE) carried bronze technology across northern Eurasia toward China's borders; **bronze metallurgy appears in China ~1700 BCE** (Erlitou). The **Indo-Iranian expansion into the Iranian plateau** runs ~2000–1500 BCE (card 206 §7). All of this is *before or during* the Mediterranean Bronze Age height — not a consequence of its fall.

So "a Central Asian center rising *as* the Med collapsed and *then* pushing into Persia and China" is **timing-inverted**: the Central Asian florescence and its outward pushes largely **precede** the Med collapse. Building the "center of gravity shifted east *because* the Med fell" argument on this risks (a) a hallucinated causal sequence and (b) missing that the real chronology actively *undercuts* the simultaneity story.

**Where the real "eastward center of gravity" belongs:** not the 1200 BCE moment, but the **Iron Age and after** — the Indo-Iranian/Median/Achaemenid rise (~1000–550 BCE) and ultimately the **Silk Road**, where card 206 already establishes the Zeravshan corridor recurring as the Transoxiana gravity-well node. That is the lasting west-and-east reorientation, downstream of the Bronze Age substrate **on its own (later) clock** and driven by the Yamnaya→Andronovo→Indo-Iranian chain (Reich/Anthony arc), *not* by Mediterranean bronze demand collapsing.

## Research Addendum (2026-07-09) — issue #35 tested against current scholarship: the timing claim is *challenged*, verdict confirms the flag

Dave asked Alpha to look for research to support or challenge the timing. A targeted literature pass **challenges the "Central Asia rising *as* the Med fell, then pushing into Persia and China" framing on every count**, and further undercuts the specific Central-Asia→Med tin link:

- **BMAC "industrial center": peaks ~2400–1700 BCE, declines ~1700–1500.** Urban/Integration era dated ~2400–1950 (Salvatori) or ~2250–1700 (Dubova/Lyonnet); decline ~1700–1500 BCE. Predates the ~1200 collapse by **300–500 years.** → *rising-as-Med-fell = false.*
- **Central Asian tin mining (Mushiston, Zeravshan/Karnab): exploited late-3rd-millennium → peak ~1700–1400 BCE → declining through the developed Late Bronze Age.** Worked by Andronovo communities, traded to BMAC. So the tin economy was **waning, not rising,** by the time of the ~1200 collapse. → *not a center building toward a push; a center already contracting.*
- **Seima-Turbino eastward metallurgy / bronze to China: ~2300–1700 BCE**; Qijia–Seima-Turbino ties ~2200–2000 BCE at the origin of Chinese metallurgy. Predates 1200 by **500–1000 years.** → *eastward push = real but far earlier.*
- **The Central-Asia→Mediterranean tin link is contested and probably minority.** Powell et al. 2022 (*Science Advances*) argued ~1/3 of the **Uluburun** (~1320 BCE) tin came from **Mushiston** (Tajikistan) + Taurus. **Berger et al. 2023 (*Frontiers in Earth Science*) rebut it**: chemical + lead-isotope incompatibility, tin isotopes can't pinpoint a source, and — the striking fact — **"the latest textual indicator for an eastern origin of tin in the Near East predates the Uluburun wreck by ~400 years"** (i.e., the *eastern* tin trade references dry up ~1720 BCE). They favor **SW England (Cornwall)**, with Sardinian and Taurus candidates also in play. → *by ~1200 the Med's tin headwaters had shifted **west**, not to Central Asia.*

**Net for the framework (stronger, as usual after a correction):** the Central Asian metallurgical fluorescence and its Persia/China pushes are an **early-to-mid 2nd-millennium** phenomenon (~2300–1500 BCE), *contracting* by the Late Bronze Age — the opposite of "rising as the Med fell." And the Late Bronze Age Med drew its tin increasingly from the **west** (Cornwall/Sardinia/Taurus), so the ~1200 collapse is a western-tin-dependent consumer node failing — the **node-failure reading holds, but the resource headwaters relink west, not to Central Asia.** The genuine eastward "center of gravity" remains an **Iron Age / Silk Road** story (card 206's Zeravshan recursion), on its own later clock. Issue #35's core question — "was any Central Asian center *growing* c. 1300–1100 BCE?" — answers **no; it was declining.** Left open for Dave's formal resolution + a check against Cunliffe's own tin-route text.

## Refinement (2026-07-09, cont.) — Cunliffe confirms Atlantic-facing (issue #35 closed); the thin *shared* tin-lifeline as the synchronicity mechanism; East–West power-projection seesaw; the thin diffusion channel

**Cunliffe check — closes issue #35.** Dave: all of Cunliffe's other bronze coverage is **Atlantic-facing** — the Carpathian metal sources, running up into **Ireland** — westward toward the Atlantic, and Cunliffe provides **no narrative prose or evidence establishing a strong Central Asia linkage.** This confirms the Research Addendum from Cunliffe's own text (the LBA tin headwaters relink *west*, not Central Asia). **Issue #35 resolved: the timing/causality claim is not supported; the Med's bronze world faced the Atlantic.**

**The strengthened mechanism (the jewel of this refinement) — a thin *shared* lifeline explains the collapse's synchronicity.** Tin was a **long, thin, single-bottleneck supply line with no redundancy**, and — crucially — **the *same* thin dependency was shared across every Mediterranean and European tin-consuming power center.** So when the lifeline was severed (by whatever shock), the consumers didn't fail independently and one-by-one; **they fell away *together*, as correlated failures of a single shared point-of-failure.** This gives the framework a concrete mechanism for the Bronze Age Collapse's notorious **synchronicity** — the "why did so many centers collapse at once?" puzzle that the "perfect storm" literature (card 311) leaves as coincidence. The framework's answer: *not N independent collapses, but one shared bottleneck severing.* Consistent with risk-invisible fragility (cards 311/312): the thin lifeline is the **fragility/amplifier that converts a localized shock into network-wide collapse**, not necessarily the initiating cause — the shock is still multi-causal (drought, unrest, raiders), and the causation likely runs both ways (unrest severs the line; the severed line deepens the unrest).

**The resource interior was insulated — material confirmation of card 314.** Central Asia and China were **moderately unaffected by this supply-severance** because they were **not dependent on the thin long-distance line** — they sat on proximate resources (Central Asian tin districts; China's own bronze industry). So the collapse is a property of the **long-distance-tin-*consumer* web** (Mediterranean + Europe), not of the resource-holding east. This is card 314's "dark age is regional to the silenced channel, not global" made *material*: the very geography of the tin network draws the boundary of the collapse. (Central Asia's *own* metallurgical decline is a separate, earlier trajectory — not caused by the Med collapse.)

**East–West power-projection seesaw (card 312).** With Central Asia past peak / declining (established above), the seesaw plus card 314's "Shang China at its height" supports Dave's inference that **China was at or near a power *maximum* ~1200 BCE** — the late Shang / Anyang (Yinxu) florescence. Held as a *probabilistic inference from the projection-curve logic*, not a hard claim: one node's projection turning down does not mechanically raise another's, but where the resource base is insulated and the rival center is contracting, the relative-power gradient tilts east. Worth testing against Shang chronology directly.

**The thin diffusion channel (Rogers / diffusion lens).** Even with bulk trade networks severed, ideas keep passing East↔West through a **very thin channel — a handful of high-risk, long-range explorer-type individuals**, the extreme far tail of the risk-taking distribution (cards 026 great-men-as-high-leverage-draws; Rogers's innovator category). High friction doesn't close the channel to zero; it **narrows transmission to the rare extreme-risk-taker tail.** Low-bandwidth, nonzero coupling — which is why technologies and motifs still leak across Eurasia during periods with no bulk exchange. This is the diffusion lens operating at minimum bandwidth: the channel width is set by how far into the risk-taking tail you must go before someone will make the crossing.
