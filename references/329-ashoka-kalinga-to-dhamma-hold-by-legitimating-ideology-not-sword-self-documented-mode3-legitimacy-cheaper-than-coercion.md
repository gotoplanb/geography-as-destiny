## The case
Ashoka (r. ~268–232 BCE), grandson of Chandragupta Maurya, inherited an empire **unified by the sword** — Chandragupta's conquest plus the Kautilyan/*Arthashastra* coercive-administrative machine. Ashoka expanded it further, culminating in the **brutal conquest of Kalinga (~262–261 BCE)**. He then **pivots within a single reign** from conquest-by-sword to governance-by-**Dhamma** — a broadcast civic-ethical code (non-violence, tolerance, welfare, respect across sects) promulgated through **rock and pillar edicts** across the empire, in local scripts and languages (Prakrit/Brahmi, Kharoshthi in the northwest, even **Greek and Aramaic** at the Kandahar fringe). **Major Rock Edict XIII documents the Kalinga remorse in his own words** — the reasoning for the shift, carved into stone.

## Framework move 1 — this is card 217's thesis, in the ruler's own words
Card 217: *you can unify by force; you can only hold by legitimating ideology* (Qin unifies China by coercion, collapses within 15 years of the founder's death; Han holds it by adding Confucian legitimacy). **Ashoka is the Indian instance — and uniquely, he self-documents it.** Chandragupta + Kautilya **unified** by sword; Ashoka **holds** by Dhamma. The framework usually has to *infer* the instrumental-religion move (Constantine, the Uyghur Manichaean adoption, Han Confucianism reconstructed from downstream effects); here the **sender left the record** — the edicts *are* the ruler's own encoding of "I stop conquering and rule by moral authority instead." That makes Ashoka the project's **best-evidenced MODE-3 case** (Beta's point), and a Shannon/sender-side gift: for once the instrumental-legitimation reasoning is on the channel directly, not reconstructed from noise.

## Framework move 2 — Dave's "way easier": legitimacy scales cheaper than coercion
The economic core of "easier to hold area by religion than sword": **coercion is expensive and localized; legitimacy is cheap and broadcast.** The sword requires garrisons, continuous rebellion-suppression, and pays a recurring blood-cost — and every unit of reach is a **power-projection cost** (card 312) that rises with distance. Moral authority / a shared civic ethic is a **low-marginal-cost, high-reach holding technology**: once the Dhamma is broadcast and internalized, subjects police themselves and the empire holds without a soldier on every road. Religion-as-holding-tech **collapses the projection cost per unit area held** — which is exactly why the switch is "easier." (Not *free*: the edict apparatus and the *dhamma-mahamatras* — the officials who propagated it — were a real investment; but a fraction of the cost of holding Kalinga-by-occupation forever.)

## Framework move 3 — Dhamma as cross-cultural middleware (card 216)
A diverse subcontinental empire — many peoples, languages, and a Hellenistic/Aramaic-speaking northwest — needs a **shared reference that reduces "cultural impedance"** (card 216's concept: the interpretive-framework mismatch that makes cross-cultural transactions fail). Ashoka's **Dhamma was deliberately ecumenical** — a civic ethic (tolerance among sects, non-violence, welfare) broader than narrow Buddhist doctrine — and the **multilingual, multi-script edicts literally publish the interoperability protocol** across the empire's diversity (Greek and Aramaic at the edge so the Hellenized frontier could read it in its own terms). This is 216's cultural-middleware made concrete and, again, self-documented.

## Framework move 4 — the Chandragupta / Ashoka pairing (with card 328)
One dynasty, **two opposite relationships to religion**, two generations apart:
- **Chandragupta (grandfather):** personal renunciation — *abandons* power for Jain asceticism (the **exception** to MODE 3; card 328 — and itself late/contested tradition).
- **Ashoka (grandson):** instrumental Dhamma — *keeps* power and rules through religious-moral authority (the **canonical MODE 3**).
A shared "**violence-of-conquest → religious turn**" motif links them, but with **opposite outputs**: the grandfather exits the state; the grandson stays and re-tools how the state holds itself together. The pairing is a clean teaching diptych — the rule and its exception, in the same bloodline. (Chandragupta's half is legendary; Ashoka's is epigraphically documented — so lean the weight on Ashoka.)

## Calibration / open items
1. **Sincere and instrumental are not either/or.** The Kalinga remorse (Rock Edict XIII) can be *genuine* and *also* have produced an instrumentally-effective governance ideology. The framework reads the **structural function** (Dhamma held a diverse empire cheaply) without adjudicating Ashoka's inner sincerity — the card-225 discipline (address what the substrate/policy produced, not the definitional/psychological question). Don't reduce it to cynical calculation *or* to pure conversion.
2. **Don't overstate the "sudden horrified conversion."** The edict documents remorse and a policy turn; the suddenness/completeness of a personal conversion is debated, and **Dhamma is a civic ethic broader than Buddhist doctrine** (Ashoka governed a multi-religious empire, not a theocracy). Keep "Dhamma" distinct from "Buddhism."
3. **Legitimacy extended duration; it wasn't permanent.** The Mauryan Empire fragmented and fell (~185 BCE, Shunga coup) within ~50 years of Ashoka. Consistent with 217 — legitimating ideology sustains *longer* than pure coercion (Mauryan ~135 yr vs. Qin 15) but is not eternal. Don't overclaim that Dhamma made the empire durable indefinitely; claim that it **lowered the cost of holding during and somewhat beyond his reign.**
4. **Dhamma wasn't costless** (edict programme + *dhamma-mahamatra* officialdom); the claim is *cheaper than* continuous military occupation, not free.
5. **Verify Cunliffe's actual treatment** of Ashoka before prose; the Kalinga/Dhamma/edict facts here are standard mainstream history (well-attested), but confirm what Cunliffe specifically says.
