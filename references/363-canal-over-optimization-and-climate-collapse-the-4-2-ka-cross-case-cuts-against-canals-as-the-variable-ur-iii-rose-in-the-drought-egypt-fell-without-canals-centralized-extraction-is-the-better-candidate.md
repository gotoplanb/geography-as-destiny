## The idea, and where it already lives

The over-optimization apparatus is already built:
- **Card 237:** risk is invisible during optimization, so a system looks healthiest just before it shatters.
- **Card 238:** over-optimizing to local conditions traps a society in a local minimum. The cultural framework can act as a resilience penalty, the way regularization does in machine learning.
- **Card 239:** order is heat death, and the mature goal is graceful transition.
- **Card 310:** applied the same reading to Minoan Crete.

**The Chinese case is the Southern Song at Hangzhou, not Beijing.** Hangzhou specialized so heavily in commerce that it imported grain (card 235). One correction matters here: the Song's breaking shock was the Mongol conquest (1279), not climate. Dave's Mesopotamian version is climate-triggered, so it is a new claim, not a re-run of the Song cards.

**"The people are still there" is the project's position already.** Card 313 calls 1200 BCE a node failure, not a system collapse. Watch for Scott making the same argument in his later chapter on the fragility of early states.

## The cross-case test: the 4.2 ka event (c. 2200–1900 BCE)

This is the best natural test in the region: one climate shock hitting states that differed in how much they depended on canals.

| State | Water regime | Outcome under the 4.2 ka drought |
|---|---|---|
| Akkad (c. 2334–2154 BCE) | Rain-fed northern heartland (Habur), plus the south | Collapses c. 2154 BCE (Weiss et al. 1993) |
| Egypt, Old Kingdom | Natural spine; basin irrigation, not trunk canals | Collapses c. 2181 BCE; low Nile floods (Bell 1971) → First Intermediate Period |
| Ur III (c. 2112–2004 BCE) | Canal-dependent south; the most centralized and administered of the three | **Rises during the drought**; falls a century later |

**Canal dependence does not sort the outcomes the way the prediction says.** The states that relied least on canals fell first. The most canal-dependent state was founded in the middle of the drought, plausibly *because* irrigation buffered it against rainfall failure. Canals were drought insurance before they were a brittleness.

**How Ur III actually fell** (c. 2004 BCE, multiple causes):
- grain shortages and soaring prices at Ur under Ibbi-Sin;
- Amorite pressure — a wall was built against them — and plausibly migrants displaced by the drought in the north;
- defections of provinces;
- an Elamite sack.

The successors were the Isin and Larsa dynasties, then Amorite Babylon: same land, largely the same people, new rulers. That fits the "people persist" half cleanly.

## Two versions that could survive

1. **Canals buy time, then fail on second-order effects.** These are reduced river discharge (the Tigris and Euphrates are fed by Anatolian and Zagros rain, so drought eventually reaches the canal head), refugee inflow, and slow salinization (card 362; Jacobsen & Adams 1958, contested by Powell 1985). This keeps canals in the story, but as a delay mechanism: the over-optimized system outlasts its neighbours and then fails harder.
2. **The variable is centralized extraction, not canals.** Ur III is the most bureaucratically centralized state of the three, with very large administrative archives and a rotating provincial tax system (*bala*). Card 343's Neo-Assyria result says extraction during expansion erodes its own base and produces total collapse. On that reading, canals are just the channel the extraction runs through.

## The circularity guard

"Over-optimized" is easy to see after a collapse. Card 237's own logic means the risk is invisible beforehand, so the claim needs a measure that can be taken *before* the outcome is known. Candidate measures:
- dependence on a single crop (barley share);
- administrative density per unit of extraction (which links to card 357's prediction);
- share of calories from irrigated land;
- the reach of the canal network relative to the natural channels.

If none can be measured before the collapse, the claim is unfalsifiable and stays an interpretation.
