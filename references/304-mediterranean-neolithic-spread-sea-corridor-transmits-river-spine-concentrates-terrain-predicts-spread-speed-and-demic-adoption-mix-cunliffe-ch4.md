# The Mediterranean Neolithic Spread (Greece → Portugal in ~600 Years) — The Sea Corridor *Transmits*, the River Spine *Concentrates* / Terrain Predicts Both the Spread *Speed* and the Demic/Adoption *Mechanism Mix* / Cunliffe Ch. 4 + Framework Prediction

**Source:** Beta seminar discussion, 2026-06-29, during Dave's listening of Barry Cunliffe, *Europe Between the Oceans: 9000 BC – AD 1000* (Yale University Press, 2008), Chapter 4 (the Mediterranean Neolithic spread, Greece to Portugal in ~600 years; chapter per Dave's listening)
**Type:** prediction / framework synthesis — a spread-geometry typology (sea corridor vs. river spine vs. varied interior); includes an evidence-strength flag (see Calibration 1 / GitHub issue)
**Relevant to:** Chapter 2 (Neolithic spread), the friction-collapse lens (low-friction corridors), the diffusion lens (demic vs. adoption), the river-spine / fixed-attractor cluster (047, 070, 277, 301), the interior-Neolithic cards (302, 303), maritime low-friction (066, 299, 300)

## Content

Cunliffe (per Dave's listening) reports that the Neolithic package spread along the Mediterranean from **Greece to Portugal in roughly 600 years** — strikingly fast. He is candid that the *mechanism* is uncertain: it could be true colonization (people moving) or adoption of the Neolithic package by populations already there. Writing in 2008, before the aDNA record, mechanistic agnosticism is the honest archaeological position.

Dave's prediction supplies the terrain logic Cunliffe lacks. Two pieces:

**1. The mechanism mix (Dave's first pass: ~20% demic pioneers / ~80% local adoption).** A thin layer of frontier people maritime-hopping around the Mediterranean basin, with the bulk of the spread being existing populations adopting the package. *(The specific proportion is flagged in Calibration 1 — the aDNA likely inverts it, and the inversion sharpens the framework. The robust claim is the terrain mechanism below, not the number.)*

**2. The geometry — why it was so fast.** The Mediterranean spread was fast because the basin offered **a good resource base among existing peoples but no vertically organized river feature to make people stop and build surplus agriculture along the way.** This is the sharp insight. Two contrasting terrain geometries:

- **The sea corridor *transmits*.** The Mediterranean littoral is a *horizontal, low-friction corridor*. A population with basic seafaring can move Greece → Italy → southern France → Iberia faster by water than the same distance overland through varied terrain. The sea moves people and the package *through* the landscape. Nothing concentrates them; they flow.
- **The river spine *concentrates*.** A vertical river feature (the Nile of card 047; the Danube as the interior LBK's organizing axis) is a *fixed linear attractor* that traps population, builds surplus, and anchors settlement. A spine makes people *stop* — and stopping is what produces surplus-agriculture concentration and, eventually, administrative complexity.

The Mediterranean spread is fast *because it lacks a concentrator*. There is no spine to stop the flow, so the wavefront runs the length of the basin in 600 years — fast but shallow (flow-through), as against the slower, anchored, surplus-building dynamic a river spine imposes.

This yields a **three-way spread-geometry typology**:
- **Sea corridor (horizontal, transmitting):** fastest spread, flow-through, thin and mobile.
- **River spine (vertical, concentrating):** slower, anchored, surplus-building, state-tending.
- **Varied interior, no corridor (cards 302/303):** slowest, fragmented into local pulses and adoption patterns; no highway, so the three-day pulse and local adoption dominate.

## Why this matters

**1. It extends the river-spine cluster by naming its inverse.** The framework's river-spine cards (047 Saharan pump, 070/277 energy gradient, 301 fixed dense resource → concentration) all describe terrain that *concentrates* population. This card names the **complementary geometry**: a corridor that *transmits* without concentrating. Same friction logic, opposite settlement consequence. The river spine is a fixed attractor (population pools and intensifies); the sea corridor is a conductor (population flows through). Holding both gives the framework a complete account of how linear terrain features shape spread — concentrate vs. transmit — rather than only the concentration half.

**2. It unifies the two Neolithic spread routes under one terrain law.** Cards 302/303 read the *interior* (Danubian/LBK) spread; this card reads the *Mediterranean* spread. One law covers both: **the spread's speed and mechanism are set by the friction geometry of the available corridor.** Sea highway → fast, demic-capable. River spine → anchored, surplus-building. No corridor → slow, adoption-fragmented. The two routes are not separate stories but two outputs of the same terrain function.

**3. It is a falsifiable prediction about *mechanism mix*, not just speed — exactly the kind Charlie asked the framework to make.** The framework predicts that terrain sets not only *how fast* the Neolithic spread but *by what mechanism* (movement vs. adoption), and that the mix varies by corridor type. That is testable against aDNA — and the test is instructive precisely where it corrects the first-pass proportion (Calibration 1). A framework that yields a checkable proportion, gets partly corrected, and emerges sharper is the framework working as intended.

## Calibration

> **RESOLVED 2026-06-29 (issue #33 closed).** The aDNA confirms the framework's directional reading: the Mediterranean maritime route was **demic-dominant — roughly 80% replacement** of sparse local hunter-gatherer ancestry by incoming Anatolian-farmer ancestry. So the correct split is ~**80% demic / 20% local**, the *inverse* of Dave's first pass. The framework's *mechanism* prediction (maritime friction collapse → rapid coastal demic spread) was right all along. **Where the error actually entered:** not in the framework read but in a bad *substrate assumption* — Dave initially assumed a dense pre-existing sedentary population (so maritime settlers were only a 20% addition). In fact the Mediterranean basin ~5500 BC held *sparse foragers*, so the maritime settlers *were* the primary population event (~80%). Methodological lesson worth keeping: the framework generated the correct directional prediction; the failure point was an empirical population-baseline assumption layered on top of it — check who was already there before assigning a ratio. Read Calibration 1–3 below as the original flag that drove this resolution.

1. **EVIDENCE-STRENGTH FLAG (now resolved — see box above) — the 20/80 demic/adoption proportion runs *opposite* to the aDNA, and the correction strengthens the framework.** Beta endorsed the 20% demic / 80% adoption split as "consistent with the aDNA." That endorsement is too generous. The weight of Reich-era ancient DNA (Haak et al. 2015; Mathieson et al. 2015; Lipson et al. 2017 on the Iberian transect) indicates the **initial** Neolithic transition across *both* routes was **substantially demic**: early European farmers — Danubian *and* Mediterranean/Cardial — derive the majority of their ancestry from Anatolian-derived farmers, with local hunter-gatherer (WHG) ancestry **low at first and *rising* over subsequent millennia** (the HG resurgence), varying by region. So a literal "80% local adoption" reading is inverted for the pioneer phase: the first farmers were mostly incomers, and adoption/admixture grew *afterward*.

   Crucially, the correction makes the framework **cleaner, not weaker** (the Tecumseh pattern): Dave's own mechanism — *maritime pioneer hopping* — is itself a **demic** process (cf. Zilhão's maritime pioneer colonization model). Followed consistently, friction-collapse logic predicts the **sea corridor produces the *more* demic spread** (the low-friction highway lets people actually move and colonize), while the **varied interior produces the *more* adoption-based spread** (no highway → locals adopt from frontier contact). That is the inverse of the 20/80-on-the-coast number — and it both fits the aDNA better and resolves the internal tension below. The differential (coast vs. interior differ in mechanism) is the robust, framework-defining claim; the *direction* of the coastal proportion is what needs flipping.

2. **Internal tension to resolve: maritime hopping is demic, so it fights the "80% adoption" number.** Dave invokes maritime hopping (people moving by boat) *and* 80% adoption (package moving faster than people) in the same breath. These pull opposite ways. If pioneers hop by sea and seed the coast, the coastal spread is demic by construction. The resolution (per Calibration 1): keep the maritime-hopping mechanism, drop the coastal 80%-adoption figure. The sea is the demic route; the interior is the adoption route.

3. **What is robust vs. fragile — state both precisely.** *Robust:* (a) the Mediterranean spread was fast; (b) the sea is a transmitting low-friction corridor while a river spine is a concentrating attractor; (c) terrain sets both spread speed and mechanism mix; (d) coast and interior differ in mechanism. *Fragile / corrected:* the specific 20/80 split and the claim that the *coast* was adoption-dominated. Do not let the typology's strength borrow credibility for the proportion, and do not let the wrong proportion discredit the typology.

4. **Cunliffe's agnosticism is honest and now resolvable.** Cunliffe (2008) could not choose between colonization and adoption because the aDNA did not yet exist. Reich-era work resolves it: *both* operated, demic dominant in the pioneer phase, proportions varying by region and terrain. This is a clean case of the framework's "literature describes; aDNA + framework explain and predict" division of labor (cards 297, 301).

5. **Probabilistic frame.** Terrain geometry *raises the probability* of a fast demic spread along the sea corridor and a slower adoption-led spread in the corridor-less interior; it does not determine either. Same distributional language as cards 010 / 301 / 302 / 303.
