## Name the magnitude first: which gap? (verified 2026-11-06)

**Scott's gap runs from domestication to agricultural *economies*, not to states.** His wording, from the Anarchist Library edition of *Against the Grain* (page numbers not checked against the Yale print edition):
- **Preface:** "both sedentism and domestication were in place at least four millennia before anything like agricultural villages appeared."
- **"Minding the Gap"** (Ch. 1): "the breathtaking four-millennia gap between the first appearance of domesticated grains and animals" and "the coalescing of agro-pastoral societies we have associated with early civilization." He restates it as "four thousand years, or roughly 160 generations." He cites Zeder for mixed subsistence economies persisting "for 4,000 years or more before the crystallization of agricultural economies."
- **"Concentration and Sedentism":** founder crops and animals domesticated "between 8,000 and 6,000 BCE," with the gap to "the first agrarian economies" now "reckoned to stretch for 4,000 years."
- **States come later still:** the first agrarian kingdoms "around 3,500 BCE." The Introduction puts the first states at "only around 3,100 BCE, more than four millennia after the first crop domestications and sedentism," and "several millennia after the first evidence of agriculture and sedentism."

**So there are two intervals, and the ordering between them is load-bearing:**
1. **Domestication → agrarian economy:** about four millennia (Scott, after Zeder). Scott's own explanation is portfolio rationality: diverse foraging was safer than planting, and other stores (livestock, fish, fermentation) reduced the need for crops. That is **blocker 3** in card 325's taxonomy (rational laggards; cards 299, 307), not beneficiary mismatch.
2. **Agrarian economy → state:** possibly only **centuries** (agrarian economies by about 4000 BCE, states 3500–3100 BCE). If so, that is the fast takeoff the complement prediction expects.

**Implication for this card:** beneficiary mismatch is a candidate for interval 2, and for why interval 1 *ended*, but only if the state or its precursors pushed grain into dominance. If agrarian economies crystallized *before* any extractor existed, grain went central without a pusher, and the "pusher is the extractor" claim covers only the last step. That is a secure-ordering question (card 351; CLAUDE.md's correlation-versus-causation boundary). Scott's later chapters, which argue that states promoted grain, are where it gets tested. **Candidate for an evidence-strength issue if Scott's ordering turns out to be vague.**

## Dave's point: the archaeological endpoint is a detection threshold

Dave: Scott's dates come from what archaeology can confirm, so there have to be enough sedentary agricultural sites for the material to be findable. That's right, with one precision: **the marker for domestication is not the grinding tool.**
- **Grinding stones predate farming by a wide margin.** At Ohalo II (Sea of Galilee), starch on a ground-stone tool shows wild barley and wheat being processed about **23,000 years ago**, "at least 12,000 years before their domestication in southwest Asia." Piperno, Weiss, Holst & Nadel, "Processing of wild cereal grains in the Upper Palaeolithic revealed by starch grain analysis," *Nature* 430 (2004): 670–673, doi:10.1038/nature02734. Nadel et al., *Antiquity* (2012), confirm routine processing of wild wheat, barley and oats. Natufian mortars and pestles (from memory: c. 13,000–9,500 BCE) likewise predate domestication.
- **The endpoint is morphological domestication in the plant remains** (non-shattering rachis, grain size). Zeder's abstract says that a long period of intensifying management preceded "archaeologically visible morphological change," which makes visible change a *late* indicator. Melinda A. Zeder, "The Origins of Agriculture in the Near East," *Current Anthropology* 52, S4 (2011): S221–S235, doi:10.1086/659307.

**Consequence:** the visible start date *lags* real cultivation. Card 351's protracted domestication is the same point. Dave's survivorship instinct runs the right way: **the true gap is, if anything, longer than four millennia, not shorter.** Detection thresholds make the start late; they don't make the gap shorter.

## Rogers already names Dave's distinction, and the bias behind it

- **Centralized versus decentralized diffusion systems.** Rogers, *Diffusion of Innovations* (5th ed., 2003), Ch. 9 ("The Change Agent"), section "Centralized and Decentralized Diffusion Systems." It contrasts the classical top-down model, run by experts and change agents, with horizontal, peer-to-peer spread in which users adapt the innovation themselves. Dave's "organic diffusion without an inventor driving it" is the decentralized system. *(The chapter is confirmed from tables of contents. Page numbers differ across editions and the copies conflict, so cite by section.)*
- **Pro-innovation bias.** Rogers' own chapter of criticisms of diffusion research names it: the field studies innovations that someone wanted adopted, and successful ones at that. Beta's "the diffusion we study is the diffusion somebody was trying to cause" is this, and it is Rogers' self-critique, not an outside one. It is the Wald-bomber structure applied to diffusion research.

## The new mechanism: beneficiary mismatch

Card 325 lists four reasons a valuable technology stalls:
1. **Transmission friction (236):** it can't get there.
2. **Cognitive quarantine (085):** it's here but filed in the wrong category.
3. **Resource-marginal laggards (307 / 010):** the current resource base makes the old way better.
4. **Power-differential suppression (325):** a dominant incumbent has no need.

Grain fits none of them. It arrived, people recognized it, and they used it. It sat in a diverse subsistence portfolio for millennia. **What stalled was not adoption but centralization, and the payoff of centralizing grain accrued to a different party than the one growing it.**

- **For the grower,** relative advantage was flat or negative. Card 299 makes grain an opportunity-cost choice, and Scott's health evidence makes the narrow grain diet worse than the portfolio.
- **For an extractor,** grain is the ideal tax base: it ripens together, can be counted and stored, and is hard to hide (card 344).

**So the adopter whose payoff drives the S-curve is not the user.** Diffusion waits until a party exists that captures the payoff and can impose adoption on the users. That is a centralized diffusion system in which the change agent is the extractor. Card 356's "legibility is generative" is the same claim from the state's side: the state doesn't just tax grain, it causes there to be more grain.

**Fifth blocker:** *beneficiary mismatch.* The technology is present, recognized and in use, but its large-scale payoff goes to a party that doesn't exist yet or can't yet enforce it. It is released by a **complement** that brings that party into being.

**How it differs from its neighbours:**
- **Card 085 (compass):** the problem is recognition, and no one sees the use. With grain, everyone sees the use.
- **Card 325 (Egypt and the chariot):** the potential adopter declines from strength. Here, the adopter who would push doesn't yet exist.

## The complement lag, and the prediction

The economics precedent is the **general-purpose technology lag.** Paul David, "The Dynamo and the Computer," *American Economic Review* 80, no. 2 (May 1990, Papers & Proceedings): 355–361 *(citation verified)*. Electrification's productivity payoff waited about four decades for factories to be redesigned around it. Latency is set by the complement, not the core technology. *(The four-decade figure is from memory; the full text was not read.)*

**Candidate complements for grain,** to be checked against Scott:
- **Population pinned to rigid rivers** by the drying of the marsh web (card 362). This lowers exit cost (card 345) and raises the cost of escaping the extractor.
- **Irrigation labour,** which concentrates both people and grain.
- **Writing and accounting,** which make the harvest legible at scale. Uruk proto-cuneiform is overwhelmingly administrative.
- **Military capacity** to hold people in place.

**Prediction (testable):** a long, flat latency, then a fast takeoff once the complement arrives. Grain's share of the diet and the landscape should rise *after* state formation, tracking the state's reach rather than any nutritional payoff. The second innovation is the real trigger, as Dave predicted. This is card 158's conjunction logic applied to diffusion.

**Circularity guard:** the complement has to be dated independently of state formation. If "the complement" is just "whatever was present when the state appeared," the claim is empty.

## The compass hedge belongs on card 085 (verified 2026-11-06; carried onto 085)

- **The early date is soft.** The spoon-shaped lodestone *sinan* is Wang Zhenduo's 1940s reconstruction. Needham saw it demonstrated in 1952, and Li Shu-hua questioned its authenticity in 1954. Whether *sinan* in the Han texts (Wang Chong's *Lunheng*, c. 1st century CE) means a magnetic pointer at all is still argued. A 2022 *Journal of Dialectics of Nature* article says no; a 2023 rebuttal (doi:10.3724/shns.2023.01.001) says yes. Huang Xing (2019, *Chinese Annals of History of Science and Technology*) tested the reconstruction experimentally. "2nd century BCE" in card 085 is not supported. At most it is "Han, contested."
- **Shen Kuo (1088) describes the needle compass and magnetic declination, but not its use at sea.**
- **Zhu Yu's *Pingzhou Ketan*** (written c. 1111–1117, published 1119) is the earliest explicit record of a compass used at sea. Zhu Yu's father, Zhu Fu, held the Guangzhou maritime shipping post. **The passage itself supports card 085's "good-enough incumbent":** pilots steered by landmarks, by the stars at night and the sun by day, and used the compass *on cloudy days*. Its first attested maritime role was as a backup for when star navigation failed, which is exactly Faxian's problem.
