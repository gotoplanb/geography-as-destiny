## The clarification

Dave's "100-ish years" in card 350 was not a dating claim. He was giving an order of magnitude: the fixed policed border is counted in **hundreds** of years, not thousands. Alpha read it literally and challenged it; the challenge is withdrawn in 350. On the order-of-magnitude reading Sahlins supports him directly.

The point the looseness was setting up is the **contrast**, and that is the content here:

- **Borders → centuries.** A permeable frontier hardening into a policed line, roughly 1659 to the late nineteenth century in the Pyrenees case.
- **Domestication → millennia.** Not a moment, not an invention, not a discovery. A process running thousands of years.

Both get taught as though they were events.

## Why this matters: the strongest compression case yet

Card 348 proposes that simplified origin stories are legibility artifacts — compressions that survive because they are transmissible. Dave's observation supplies the sharpest instance of it available, because the **compression ratio is enormous and measurable**.

Domestication is taught as a flashpoint — his phrase is "an Edison light bulb moment." The actual process, in the Near Eastern cereals, involved the fixation of traits like the non-shattering rachis over something on the order of one to two thousand years of cultivation before morphological domestication was complete. A millennia-long gradient is rendered as a point. That is not a simplification of degree; it is a change of *kind*, from process to event, and it is exactly the move that produces the great-man and the Edison-moment story everywhere else.

This is also why the compression is pedagogically irresistible rather than sinister, which card 348 needs to stay honest: a gradient has no protagonist and no date, and is therefore nearly unteachable at grade-school level. Note the connection to Vansina's floating gap in card 350 — beyond living memory there is only origin-time, and origin-time admits no gradients.

## The climate-forcing half is not new — it is already load-bearing here

Dave's framing that "a lot of the domestication events were driven by a combination of geography and climate... versus just being random" is **already this project's established position**, and he should know how much machinery sits under it before restating it as a fresh thought:

- The prologue's "we survived agriculture, we didn't invent it"
- Card 047, the Saharan pump — agriculture forced
- Cards 299 and 300, marginality and opportunity cost as the mechanism
- Card 301, Natufian sedentism as thermodynamic
- Card 306, the Baltic holdout, which the project treats as its *clean* natural experiment
- Card 323, which flags the Younger Dryas version of the claim as **contested** and opened evidence-strength issue #36

That last one is the live caution and it applies here unchanged. The general claim — climate and geography forced rather than permitted agriculture — is well supported. The flagship Younger Dryas case is not, and 323 redirects to 306 for the clean version. Do not let the restatement quietly re-adopt the contested case.

**The one source worth adding:** Richerson, Boyd and Bettinger, "Was Agriculture Impossible during the Pleistocene but Mandatory during the Holocene? A Climate Change Hypothesis," *American Antiquity* (2001). It argues agriculture was impossible under last-glacial conditions and became compulsory in the Holocene — the "mandatory" half is the prologue's position stated in the literature, and it is a stronger anchor for the general claim than the Younger Dryas case 323 flagged.
