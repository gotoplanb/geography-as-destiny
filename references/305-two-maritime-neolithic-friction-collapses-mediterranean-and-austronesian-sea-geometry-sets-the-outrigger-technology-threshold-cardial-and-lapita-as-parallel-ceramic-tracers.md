# Two Maritime-Neolithic Friction Collapses — Mediterranean and Austronesian / Sea Geometry Sets the *Outrigger Technology Threshold* / Cardial and Lapita as Parallel Ceramic Tracers

**Source:** Beta seminar discussion, 2026-06-29, during Dave's listening of Barry Cunliffe, *Europe Between the Oceans: 9000 BC – AD 1000* (Yale University Press, 2008), Ch. 4 — Dave connecting the Mediterranean Neolithic spread to the contemporaneous-in-kind Austronesian maritime expansion
**Type:** connection / framework refinement — friction collapse is *relative to the terrain's baseline friction*; the enabling technology threshold is set by sea geometry
**Relevant to:** Chapter 2 (Neolithic spread), the friction-collapse lens (threshold relative to baseline), the Pacific cascade cluster (cards 289, 290, 292), the Mediterranean spread (card 304), maritime low-friction (066, 299, 300)

## Content

Dave's connection: the Mediterranean Neolithic spread (card 304) is not a one-off. The **same kind of event** — an agricultural/ceramic package spreading rapidly by sea, demic-dominant, over sparse prior populations — happened again in **Island Southeast Asia and the Pacific** via the **Austronesian / Out-of-Taiwan expansion**. Two maritime-Neolithic friction collapses, same mechanism, different ocean.

But they are **not simultaneous**, and the difference is the whole point:

**The distinguishing variable is sea geometry, which sets the maritime-technology threshold.** A "maritime friction collapse" is not absolute — it is collapse *relative to the baseline friction the particular sea imposes*. The two seas impose very different baselines:

- **Mediterranean — enclosed, island-dense, inter-visible.** A semi-enclosed basin with short, often inter-visible hops and continuous coastlines you can hug. Its baseline maritime friction is *already low*. Existing simple craft and coasting were sufficient; **no specialized open-ocean technology was required.** The friction was low enough to collapse with what people already had — which is why the spread could run Greece→Portugal in ~600 years (card 304) without waiting on an invention.

- **Island Southeast Asia → Pacific — open ocean, long blue-water crossings.** Beyond the continental shelf, the hops are long, often over open water with no land in sight, into rising swell. The baseline friction is *high*, and coasting craft cannot collapse it. This expansion had to **wait for an enabling technology — the outrigger (and later double-hull) canoe** — which supplies the lateral stability open-water passage demands (the "outrigger friction collapse" of card 292).

**Dave's crisp formulation: "Taiwan needed outrigger technology that the Mediterranean did not."** Same package, same maritime-spread mechanism, same demic-dominant pattern over sparse predecessors — but the Pacific expansion was *gated by a technology* the Mediterranean expansion never needed, because the Pacific's sea geometry set a far higher threshold. That gate is a large part of why the Austronesian maritime spread (~3000–2000 BC into ISEA; Lapita into Remote Oceania ~1300–1000 BC) postdates the Mediterranean one (~5600–5000 BC) by millennia: the Mediterranean didn't have to wait for anything; the Pacific had to wait for the outrigger.

## Why this matters

**1. It refines the friction-collapse lens: collapse is *relative to baseline friction*, and the technology threshold is terrain-set.** This is a real sharpening (per Charlie's ask to sharpen friction-collapse boundaries). "Friction collapse" can be achieved with *existing* technology where the baseline is already low (Mediterranean), or can *require a specific invention* where the baseline is high (the Pacific outrigger). The terrain — here, sea geometry (enclosed/island-dense vs. open-ocean) — sets *how much* technology the collapse demands. The lens is not "new tech → collapse" but "terrain sets the threshold; collapse happens when available technology clears it." The Mediterranean cleared it with coasting; the Pacific had to invent its way over the bar.

**2. It contextualizes card 292's "outrigger friction collapse" — the outrigger is not universally necessary, it is geometry-specific.** Card 292 treats the outrigger as the Pacific's enabling friction-collapse technology. Card 305 shows *why that requirement is not general*: the Mediterranean accomplished the homologous spread without it. The outrigger is the answer to a *high-threshold sea*, not a universal prerequisite for maritime expansion. This prevents over-generalizing the Pacific case into a false "all maritime spread needs blue-water craft" rule.

**3. The demic-dominant-over-sparse-population pattern repeats — and escalates — across both cases.** Card 304's resolution: the Mediterranean spread was ~80% demic because the basin held only *sparse foragers* (the maritime settlers were the primary population event). The Pacific case is the *same pattern in extremis*: Near Oceania was thinly populated, and **Remote Oceania was virgin — the Lapita people were the first humans ever to arrive.** So the "demic-dominant because few or no people were already there" condition runs from ~80% (Mediterranean) to ~100% (Remote Oceania). One pattern, two intensities, set by how empty the destination was. This is the substrate-baseline lesson of issue #33 generalized: always check who was already there.

**4. Cardial/Impressed Ware ↔ Lapita: parallel ceramic tracers of two maritime farming expansions.** Dave's note that this was "a Neolithic expansion of pottery in the maritime" lands a clean parallel. Both expansions are tracked archaeologically by a **distinctive decorated-pottery horizon**: Cardial/Impressed Ware in the Mediterranean, Lapita (and its red-slipped ISEA antecedents) in the Pacific. Card 290 already reads Lapita as "cultural DNA" — a material-culture signal confirming population connection. Card 305 makes Cardial its Mediterranean homologue: two decorated-ware horizons, each the encoded material signature of a maritime agricultural diffusion. The pottery is the tracer dye in both oceans.

## Calibration

1. **Parallel in kind, not simultaneous — mind the offset.** Mediterranean Cardial/Impressed Ware ~5600–5000 BC; Austronesian Out-of-Taiwan into ISEA ~3000–2000 BC; Lapita into Remote Oceania ~1300–1000 BC. These are *homologous mechanisms separated by millennia*, not one contemporaneous event (Beta's correction, correct). The offset is itself explained by the outrigger gate (Calibration 2).

2. **Outrigger origin/dating is debated; the *threshold* claim is robust regardless.** The precise date and place of the outrigger/double-hull canoe's invention are not settled. The framework reads the outrigger as the enabling open-water technology consistent with card 292, but the load-bearing claim here does not depend on an exact chronology: it is that **open-ocean ISEA/Pacific demanded blue-water craft that the enclosed Mediterranean did not.** That geometric difference holds whatever the outrigger's exact origin date. State the threshold claim firmly; hold the outrigger-dating loosely.

3. **"Same package" is an analogy of mechanism, not of crops.** The Mediterranean and Austronesian Neolithic packages differ in content (Southwest-Asian cereals/sheep/goats vs. ISEA rice/millet/tubers/pigs/chickens/dogs). The parallel is in the *mechanism* (maritime demic spread of an agricultural-ceramic package over sparse predecessors), not in the specific domesticates. Keep the homology at the mechanism level.

4. **Probabilistic frame.** Sea geometry *raises or lowers the probability* that an available technology clears the threshold for rapid maritime spread; it does not determine the outcome. Enclosed seas make early collapse likely; open oceans make it contingent on a specific invention. Same distributional language as cards 010 / 304.
