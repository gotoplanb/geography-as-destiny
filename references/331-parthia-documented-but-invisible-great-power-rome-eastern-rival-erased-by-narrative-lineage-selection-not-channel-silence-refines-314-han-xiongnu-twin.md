## The fact Dave caught
The **Parthian (Arsacid) Empire, ~247 BC – 224 AD**, ruled from the Mediterranean to India for nearly **five centuries** and was **Rome's primary and most persistent eastern rival** — a *sustained* existential rival far longer than Carthage (destroyed 146 BC). At **Carrhae (53 BC)**, the Parthian general Surenas's horse-archers and cataphracts **annihilated Crassus's legions and took the eagles** — one of Rome's worst defeats. Rome never conquered Parthia; the rivalry shaped the eastern frontier (Armenia, Mesopotamia) for ~300 years, and its **successor, the Sassanids (224 AD onward), continued the Rome/Byzantium–Persia rivalry for another ~400 years.** By any measure a first-rank Old World power — and essentially **invisible in popular Western historical memory.** Dave's instinct is exactly right and well-founded.

## Why it's invisible — and why that's *not* card 314's mechanism
This is a clean sender-side/Wald case, but it is **the opposite of a silent channel.** The record on Parthia is *abundant* — it just isn't *Parthia's own, and it isn't on the Western lineage-line.* Two layers:

**Layer 1 — Parthia wrote no history of itself (Shannon sender-side; extends card 131).** The Parthians **were literate but produced no dynastic histories.** What survives in their *own* voice is coins, ostraca, rock inscriptions, scattered parchments — not narrative history. So Parthia is known almost entirely **from the outside**: Greek and Roman histories (hostile), plus **Chinese histories** (which knew Parthia as *"Anxi"*), plus later Sassanid material. This is card **131's "the nomadic worldview never got its Plato" — but extended to a *literate, sedentary* empire.** The striking upgrade: it's not only *nomads* who fail to encode themselves in the durable narrative genre; **even a settled, literate great power can decline to write its own history and thereby survive only through its enemies' pens.** Parthia "never got its Tacitus." (Modern scholars literally reconstruct the Parthian king-list by combining hostile Greco-Roman texts with **numismatic evidence** — the framework's "read the other instruments" move is how the field actually works.)

**Layer 2 — the Western lineage-narrative selects the *western* frontier (cards 170/131/132).** Even the Roman record that *does* cover Parthia gets buried downstream, because Western popular history is a **lineage story**, not a neutral record. It foregrounds the rivals on the line to "us": **Carthage** (the Punic Wars where Rome *became* the Mediterranean empire — foundational to the Western story), the **Gauls and Germans** (where France/Germany trace their origins and where the *Western* empire fell into medieval Europe), **Constantinople and the split** (the road to Christendom). The **eastern** rivalry leads to **Persia / Islam / "the East"** — *off* the lineage-line — so it's compressed into footnotes. This is the **origin-myth / naming-impulse selection** the framework already catches for Greece (170) and Texas (132), applied to Roman historiography: the record foregrounds "our" ancestors' rivals and backgrounds "theirs."

**The refinement (why Parthia is worth its own card):** the framework's Wald cluster had (a) **channel silence** (314 — records stop; Yamnaya, dark ages) and (b) **downstream lineage-selection** (170/132 — records exist but the origin-narrative starts elsewhere). **Parthia is the clean case that isolates (b) *at maximum contrast*:** the documentation is *abundant* (three independent civilizations wrote about it — Rome, Greece, Han China — plus its own coins), and it is *still* invisible in popular memory. So Parthia proves invisibility can be **pure narrative selection with zero channel silence** — the strongest possible demonstration that the record's existence and its *memory* are different variables. It also **extends 131** from nomads to a literate sedentary empire.

## Carrhae as the Wald-bomber-on-the-record (parallel to 311)
Note the *one* sliver of Parthia that *did* survive in popular memory: **Carrhae** — and it survives precisely because it's a **Roman disaster** the Roman sources dwelt on (Crassus's humiliation, the lost eagles). We remember the **Roman wound, not the Parthian power that inflicted it.** That is card **311's Wald-bomber-on-the-record** exactly: the visible bullet-hole (Rome's defeat) is what the surviving instrument recorded; the empire behind the arrow stays invisible. Even Parthia's one famous moment is encoded as *Rome's* story, not Parthia's.

## Rome : Parthia :: Han : Xiongnu (Beta's parallel)
Parthia is the **western-Eurasian twin of the Xiongnu** (cards 211/222): the great rival known mainly through the enemy's records, under-emphasized in the "civilized" power's popular narrative. Two nuances make the pair sharp:
- The Xiongnu are the *steppe/nomadic* exemplar of 131's "never got its Plato"; **Parthia is the *sedentary, literate* exemplar** — the same epistemic gap in a settled empire, which is the more surprising (and more instructive) case.
- **Parthia sat *between* the two great record-producing civilizations** — documented by **Rome to its west and Han China (*"Anxi"*) to its east**, as the **Silk Road middleman** of the Rome–China trade — and is *still* invisible in the Western telling. The power both empires described from the outside, and neither empire's descendants centered.

## Why this matters
- **A flagship, self-verifying instance of the framework's core epistemology.** Parthia demonstrates "the record's *existence* and its *memory* are different variables" more cleanly than any case yet — abundant documentation, near-total popular invisibility — and its *recovery* (coins + archaeology + rival + third-party records) is the "read the other instruments" method in live scholarly practice.
- **Extends card 131 and sharpens the Wald cluster:** the "never got its Plato" gap is not nomad-specific; and Parthia isolates *narrative selection* from *channel silence* at maximum contrast.
- **Pedagogically potent** (like 170 for Greece): it catches the Rome-centered narrative every Western reader carries, in real time, on a case they can check.

## Calibration / open items
1. **Parthia is not *undocumented* — the claim is *self-*undocumented + *narratively* buried.** Don't say "we know little about Parthia"; say "we know it almost entirely from *outside* (Rome/Greece/China) and its *material* record (coins), because it wrote no dynastic history, and the downstream Western narrative looks away from the eastern frontier." Precision matters or the card collapses into a vague "forgotten empire."
2. **Don't overstate the invisibility as total.** Parthia is well-studied *in scholarship* (numismatics, Iranology); the claim is about *popular/curricular* Western memory, not the academic field. Scope it to the popular narrative.
3. **Carthage/Germans were genuinely consequential too** — the claim is not that they're over-remembered relative to their weight, but that Parthia is *under*-remembered relative to *its* weight, and the asymmetry tracks the lineage-line, not historical consequence.
4. **The Rome:Parthia :: Han:Xiongnu parallel is structural, not identical** — Parthia was a settled empire, the Xiongnu a steppe confederation; the shared feature is "great rival known through the enemy's record," not a common political form.
5. **Verify Cunliffe's actual treatment** of Parthia (he likely gives it real weight as a Silk Road hinge — which is part of Dave's surprise: Cunliffe restores it, the popular narrative omits it). The framing here is the framework's, not Cunliffe's.
