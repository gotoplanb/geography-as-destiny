## The verification — both sides, honestly
**Supported (Dave's instinct is real at this level):** Mauryan maritime trade existed and was **state-administered.** Kautilya's *Arthashastra* establishes a **Navadhyaksha (Superintendent of Ships)** and an admiralty department (navigation, port taxes, fishing licenses, distressed-vessel salvage); **shipbuilding was a state monopoly** (salaried shipwrights; state-owned ships leased to merchants). The ports worked — **Barygaza/Bharukachchha** (Persian Gulf trade), **Muziris** (Red Sea), **Tamralipti** (Bengal → Ceylon and Southeast Asia). **Mauryan seals and inscriptions have been found at ancient ports in Thailand and southern Vietnam**, evidencing genuine eastward maritime reach; the **Bay-of-Bengal monsoon sailing to SE Asia was already known and used** in this period. So a stable, unified Mauryan state with a maritime bureaucracy did *support and secure* real seaborne commerce — Dave is not wrong that the ordering "fitted" maritime activity.

**Timing-inverted (the correction — the *boom* is not Mauryan):** the high-volume maritime *boom* is **post-Mauryan.** "The post-Mauryan period witnessed a boom in trade… India's economy was at its peak." The Roman–India trade — **~60 Roman shipwrecks along Indian coasts; India ≈ 20–25% of Roman trade volume — is 1st–2nd century CE**, i.e. **~200+ years after the Mauryan fall (~185 BCE).** Its driver was **Roman demand + Hippalus's *western* (Arabian Sea) monsoon route (~45 CE; card 321)**, and its beneficiaries were the **Satavahanas** (Deccan) and the Tamil kingdoms — *not* the long-gone Mauryas. So "Mauryan stability → bustling maritime Silk Road" attaches the boom to the wrong era (and the "Silk Road" label is itself anachronistic for the Mauryan period — the formal Silk Road opens with Zhang Qian ~130 BCE, post-Mauryan).

## The framework sharpening (the jewel) — stability is *permissive*; demand-pull drives *volume*
The productive lesson is a refinement of the framework's own "political stabilization → commercial expansion" pattern (Pax Mongolica, the Song boom):

- **Mauryan unification + maritime bureaucracy = a *permissive supply-side condition.*** It secured and administered the plumbing — safe ports, law, shipbuilding, a state that could tax and protect trade. Necessary-ish infrastructure. But **on its own it did not produce a boom.**
- **The boom fired ~200 years later, when the *demand-pull* arrived** — Roman appetite for pepper/spices/silk plus the western monsoon route (card 321) — under *more fragmented* polities (Satavahanas, Tamils), *not* under a Mauryan-scale unification. So **producer-side political stability is neither necessary nor sufficient for the boom; the demand-pull is the driver of volume.**
- **Therefore, distinguish two things the "stability → trade" pattern conflates:** stability **secures and administers** routes (permissive), but trade **volume** is set by **demand.** Pax Mongolica *secured* the Silk Road; the *volume* on it was demand-driven at both ends. Reading a boom off producer-side stability is the error — the same shape as **card 313's tin-timing inversion** (a plausible political anchor bolted onto a boom whose timing belongs to a different era). This *strengthens* card 321: the maritime Silk Road is **demand-pulled from the consumer end**, which is exactly why Mauryan producer-side order didn't ignite it and Roman demand did.

## The axis nuance
Mauryan connectivity was strongest **overland to the Hellenistic west** (the northwest passage/Taxila, the Seleucid treaty, Ashoka's edicts explicitly naming Hellenistic kings — Seleucids, Ptolemies) **plus eastward maritime to SE Asia** (Tamralipti). The later **western maritime boom (Arabian Sea → Rome)** is the demand-pulled, post-Mauryan phenomenon. So not only the *timing* but the *axis* shifts: Mauryan = overland-west + maritime-east; the boom = maritime-west.

## Calibration / open items
1. **Don't overcorrect to "no Mauryan maritime trade."** The state maritime administration and the SE-Asia reach are real; the claim is that the *boom* (high-volume, Rome-facing) is post-Mauryan, not that the Mauryans had no seaborne commerce.
2. **Mauryan maritime volume is not precisely quantifiable** — soft archaeological/textual evidence (the *Arthashastra* is normative; seals are suggestive). Hold "real but moderate," not a number.
3. **Permissive ≠ irrelevant.** The boom *also* needed permissive supply-side plumbing at its own time (Satavahana/Tamil ports and security). The claim is that stability is permissive infrastructure, not the *driver of volume* — demand is. Both are needed; don't flip to demand-only.
4. **Stability can also *generate* demand** (a wealthy stable empire is itself a consumer — the import side). The clean claim here is about the *export* boom to Rome, which was pulled by *Roman* demand; keep that scoped.
5. **Verify Cunliffe's treatment** — does he link Mauryan stability to maritime trade, or is that Dave's synthesis? (Likely Dave's.) The "Silk Road" label is anachronistic for the Mauryan period regardless.
