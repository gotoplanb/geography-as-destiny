# "Migration of the X People" as Category Error — Polynesian Identity as Emergent From the Cascade, Not Inherited From a Source Population

**Source:** Beta seminar discussion, 2026-06-16, during Dave's listening of Christina Thompson, *Sea People: The Puzzle of Polynesia* (William Collins, 2019), Part 3
**Type:** connection
**Relevant to:** Foreword (anti-eugenics, mixture-is-fundamental), Chapter 1 (cascade derivation, mixture cluster), general methodology

## Content

A common framing in popular and even some scholarly writing: *"the migration of the Polynesian people from somewhere to the Pacific."* The framework rejects this as a category error. There were no Polynesians at the origin of the cascade. Polynesian identity — the specific language family, material culture, genetic profile, navigational tradition, social organization that European observers later encountered — emerged *through* the cascade itself.

The Austronesian expansion (Bellwood's Out-of-Taiwan model, c. 3000–2500 BCE) produced the substrate population that began the cascade. That substrate moved through the Philippines, Borneo, Indonesia; through Melanesia, where documented Melanesian admixture entered the genome; into Near Oceania over generations; through the Lapita complex; and only in the last legs of the cascade — when populations settled the remote Polynesian triangle bounded by Hawai'i, Aotearoa, and Rapa Nui — does a population become recognizable as "Polynesian" in the form Captain Cook and his successors documented.

Each stage involved mixing with prior populations. Each stage involved technological innovation — the outrigger canoe, specific navigational practices, voyaging chants tuned to specific island clusters. Each stage produced something not fully present at the previous stage. The Polynesians who greeted European ships in the 18th century were the cumulative product of three to four thousand years of population mixing and cultural innovation along the cascade route. They did not exist as a named, distinct population at the cascade's origin.

"Migration of the Polynesian people" is a sender-end abstraction projected backward from the receiver-end result. The actual mechanism was a multi-stage diffusion-and-mixing cascade that produced the receiver-end identity *emergently*. The named population is what the cascade output, not what the cascade input.

The parallels are immediate and the framework already engages them:

- **There were no Yamnaya in Old Europe.** Yamnaya-derived ancestry mixed with European farmer ancestry and earlier hunter-gatherer ancestry to produce the post-Yamnaya European populations. Those populations are not Yamnaya who relocated. They are a new emergent product of the cascade (Reich; cards 267, 268, 270).
- **There were no Han Chinese before the cascade that produced them.** Han Chinese identity emerged through cumulative integration of multiple substrate populations across centuries (card 275).
- **There were no Americans in Britain who became Americans by relocating.** American identity emerged through cascade-and-mixing on the receiving continent over centuries — and is still emerging.

The methodological principle generalizes. Replace *"migration of the X people"* with *"emergence of the X people through the cascade"* and the framework gets a much truer picture of how human populations actually formed.

This is also the substrate-level rejection of the Aryan Māori misframe documented in card 287. Tregear's error was not just identifying the wrong source population. His prior error was assuming Māori existed as a coherent population that *could* have a source in the first place. Same category error, with a different substrate.

## Why this matters

This refines the framework's anti-replacement discipline in two ways.

1. **Emergence-through-cascade as the operational alternative to migration-as-relocation.** Reich's "mixture is fundamental" (card 267) is the framework's empirical commitment. This card adds the methodological consequence: framings like "migration of the X people" need to be rejected at the level of the framing itself, because X often did not exist at the origin and so could not have migrated. The framework should look for the *cascade that produced X* rather than for the *route by which X moved*. The cascade is the mechanism. The named population is the result.

2. **Sharpens the framework's prediction for any case where current literature uses migration-of-the-X-people framing.** When mainstream or popular writing talks about ancient migrations of named populations, the framework predicts that the genetic and archaeological record, properly read, will show a cascade that produced the named population rather than the named population moving en bloc. Card 278 demonstrates the move for the Pacific cascade. The same move applies to "the Indo-European migration," "the Bantu migration," "the Anglo-Saxon migration" — each is correctly read as the cascade-and-mixing process that produced the recognizable result, not as the relocation of a pre-existing named population.

This is one of the framework's sharper methodological commitments. It is the move that lets the framework engage with deep-time population history without inheriting the category errors that pre-genetic-evidence scholarship built into the field's basic vocabulary.
