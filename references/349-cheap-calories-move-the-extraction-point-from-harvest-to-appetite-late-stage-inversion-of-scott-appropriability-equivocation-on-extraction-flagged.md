# Cheap Calories Move the Extraction Point from Harvest to Appetite — A Proposed Late-Stage Inversion of Scott's Appropriability Argument / The Equivocation on "Extraction" Has to Be Fixed First

**Source:** Dave, Beta seminar, 2026-11-02, reading James C. Scott, *Against the Grain*. Claim structure drafted by Beta in that session and reproduced below; challenges and verification added by Alpha.
**Type:** connection / speculative extension
**Relevant to:** cards 344 and 345 (appropriability, exit cost), Chapter 4 (River Spine — surplus and class structure), Chapter 11

## The claim, as drafted in the Beta session

> **Claim:** When calories are scarce and can be appropriated at one moment (grain at harvest), the binding problem for the state is apportioning and extracting them. When the cost of producing calories collapses, apportioning stops being the constraint. The extraction target shifts from the harvest to consumption, and food becomes partly recreation.
>
> **Link:** Specialization of friction on extraction (refs 344-345). Scott's grain-versus-tuber appropriability argument is the ancestral case, and this is the late-stage inversion.
>
> **Evidence handle:** NIH ultra-processed diet trial, with participants eating about 500 more kcal/day. That shows overeating, not literal hunger.
>
> **Caveats:** No coordinated design is implied, since firms maximize sales, not state labor. Distribution failures persist, so abundance is uneven.
>
> **Status:** Stub, pending Scott's later chapters.

Dave's own framing in the same session: the cost of extracting calories "has gotten so cheap that it no longer really has to be apportioned," and where abundance is extreme, calories switch "into being, we'll call it even like leisure or recreation," which is "not the original intention of calories for a sedentary state." He also observes that much modern food functions as an appetite stimulant sold to drive further purchasing, so that some food makes you hungrier — the inverse of what a calorie is supposed to do.

## Verification

**The evidence handle holds.** Hall et al., "Ultra-Processed Diets Cause Excess Calorie Intake and Weight Gain," *Cell Metabolism*, 16 May 2019. Inpatient randomized crossover, 20 adults, 14 days on each arm, diets matched for presented calories, carbohydrate, fat, sugar and salt. Ad libitum intake was **508 ± 106 kcal/day greater** on the ultra-processed arm (p = 0.0001), with ~0.9 kg gained and an equivalent amount lost on the unprocessed arm. Caveats: n = 20, two weeks per arm, a controlled inpatient setting, and the arms differed in energy density and palatability. Strong demonstration, small study.

**Scott's health argument is real but narrower than the session's gloss.** He argues early agrarian populations show a narrowed diet, reduced stature and skeletal robustness, and the diseases of sedentism and crowding — the title's wordplay runs on *parasite* as "beside the grain." But the comparison is **early farmers against foragers**, not a claim that present-day agricultural populations have the worst health on record. "Health for people who live off agriculture has never been worse" is Dave's extension, not Scott's, and should not be attributed to him.

## The challenge: "extraction" is doing two different jobs

This is the load-bearing problem and the claim should not be used until it is resolved.

In Scott, the extractor is a **state**, the extracted thing is a **storable staple**, and the mechanism is **appropriation** — someone takes a share of a visible harvest. In the modern half of the claim, the extractor is a **firm**, the extracted thing is **money**, and calories are not extracted at all. They are *pushed*. The flow reverses: the ancestral case takes grain away from the eater, the modern case pushes calories toward the eater in order to take money.

So "the extraction point moved from harvest to appetite" is elegant and may be an equivocation. Both are extraction only in a sense loose enough to cover most economic activity.

**The repair, which is probably the real claim:** what stayed constant is not the extractor but the structure — *the appropriable scarce thing determines who can build what on top of it*. When calories were scarce and legible, states formed around appropriating calories. When calories became abundant and illegible as a target, the scarce appropriable thing became attention and appetite, and the institutions that formed around appropriating *those* are firms rather than states. That is a genuine structural parallel and it survives the objection, because it stops claiming the same thing is being extracted.

Stated that way it also becomes testable against the book's own apparatus: it predicts that the institutional form follows the appropriable substrate, which is exactly what cards 344 and 345 argue for grain and for exit.

## Two further flags

- **Strip the teleology.** "The original intention of calories for a sedentary state" and "calories so individuals can do work for the society" read as functionalist. Calories were never *for* anything. Scott's claim is about what a state could appropriate, not about what food was designed to do. The argument does not need the teleology and is weaker carrying it.
- **The nutrient-density claim is contested.** "Modern food is less nutrient dense" has a real literature on declining micronutrient content in crops, but it is disputed and frequently overstated. It is not required for the argument — the Hall trial establishes overconsumption without any claim about nutrient density — so either drop it or source it properly.

Beta's own assessment, which Alpha agrees with: **"recreation" is the strongest new piece.** The shift from calories-as-input-to-labour to calories-as-consumer-good is a real change in what food is for, and it is the part of this that is not already in Scott.

## Status

Stub. Not for the book — Dave is explicit that this reading is not intended for incorporation. Revisit against Scott's later chapters, and do not use the claim until the extraction equivocation is fixed.
