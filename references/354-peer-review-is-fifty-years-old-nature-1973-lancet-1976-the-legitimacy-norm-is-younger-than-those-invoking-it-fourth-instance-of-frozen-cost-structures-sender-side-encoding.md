## The dates

- *Nature* mandated external referee reports for submitted papers in **1973**.
- *The Lancet* implemented peer review in **1976**.
- The term "peer review" was **coined in the early 1970s**.
- *Science* and *JAMA* did not use external reviewers until **after 1940**.
- Peer review did not become standard practice in the sciences until after the Second World War, reaching its current form between the **1950s and 1970s**.

**The necessary caveat, which the card should carry so the claim does not overreach.** Editorial vetting is old — the Royal Society's *Philosophical Transactions* from 1665 had something referee-like. What is recent is *external, systematic, mandatory* peer review as the gate on legitimacy. The honest claim is not "peer review was invented in 1973." It is that **the specific institution now treated as the eternal guarantor of scientific legitimacy reached its current form within living memory**, at the flagship journals, in the 1970s.

## Why this is the anchor

The structure is identical to card 353's stereotype plates: a recent arrangement, produced by the conditions of its moment, mistaken for the nature of the thing. And it is available as a *date* rather than an argument, which makes it unusually cheap to deploy. You do not have to persuade anyone that the norm is bad. You only have to say when it started.

**It applies directly to this project's own defence.** The README answers the question *is a human-AI collaboration legitimate scholarship?* The objection behind that question is an appeal to how scholarship is properly done — and the version of "properly" being invoked is younger than most of the people invoking it. That is not a refutation, because a norm's recency does not make it wrong. But it does move the burden: an institution fifty years old has to justify itself on its merits rather than on its antiquity, and the merits are arguable in a way the antiquity is not.

## Dave's three-part structure, and which part is strongest

1. **The "always been" fallacy.** An appeal to tradition resting on a tradition that is not old.
2. **The cognitive leg.** Brains prefer the simple version because decision-making is cheaper that way. This is the same mechanism as card 348's second leg, and card 350's note on Vansina's floating gap gives it a horizon — beyond living memory there is only origin-time, and origin-time admits no gradients. A fifty-year-old norm sits just far enough back to read as always.
3. **The power leg, with Dave's qualification: the tool has less influence than most people think.** This is the sharp part and the cards support it. Nobody *decided* books should be fixed — it fell out of the plate economics in 353. Nobody designed the grade-school agriculture story in 348. **Cost structures do the causal work; power arrives afterward to inherit and defend what was already cheap.** That is a weaker claim than "it is a tool of control" and much better evidenced.

That qualification also resolves an open challenge in card 348, where Alpha flagged the state-interest leg as possibly just-so and redundant to the cognitive leg. Across 347, 350, 353 and now 354 the answer is consistent: the cost structure is primary, the power interest is secondary and parasitic on it.

## The pattern, now four instances

- **347** — monuments as high-compression media. Compression is cheap; what gets dropped is contested detail.
- **350** — the fixed policed border. Line control became affordable, so states built it where terrain supplied no chokepoint.
- **353** — the fixed edition. Stereotype plates made reprinting cheap and correction expensive; fixity is the residue.
- **354** — external peer review. (Mechanism *not yet verified* — the plausible story is postwar manuscript volume, specialization beyond an editor's competence, and the need to allocate scarce funding and attention. **Check before using.**)

Four is enough to state it: **apparently natural institutions are frequently frozen cost structures, and friction collapse is what unfreezes them.** The institution outlives the economics that produced it and gets re-read as principle.

## Dave's connection: survivorship bias and sender-side encoding

His own framing, and it is the right one. Each of these is a **sender** whose encoding constraints shaped the message — the printer with expensive paper, the engraver with a limited inscription, the journal with more submissions than editorial attention. We then read the output as though it were the thing itself rather than the thing as that sender could afford to encode it. Card 347's "the absence is an artifact" is the same observation from the receiving end.

This is the project's Shannon material (033, 240) pointed at institutions rather than at terrain, and Dave expects it to be fruitful. Worth noting it also points back at him: this book is a sender with its own constraints, and the version stamp in 353 is the unusual case of a sender documenting them.
