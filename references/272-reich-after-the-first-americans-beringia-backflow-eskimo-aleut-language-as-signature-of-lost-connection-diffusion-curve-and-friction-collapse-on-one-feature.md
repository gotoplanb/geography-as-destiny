## What the Evidence Actually Shows

**The language and the population.** The cleanest match for "a Northeast Siberian language not spoken elsewhere in Asia, with the genome showing American backflow" is **Eskimo-Aleut** — specifically the **Siberian Yupik / Naukan** languages of Chukotka, members of an otherwise-American Arctic family. Reich's discussion is that **Eskimo-Aleut speakers migrated *back* to Asia, carrying "First American" genetic ancestry**; the **Naukan and coastal Chukchi of Northeast Siberia carry First American DNA**. This is the back-migration Dave heard described.

**The deeper sibling case — Dene-Yeniseian.** The more spectacular (and more contested) version of the same phenomenon is the **Dene-Yeniseian** hypothesis (Edward Vajda), linking the **Na-Dene** languages of North America (Athabaskan, Tlingit) with the **Yeniseian** languages of Siberia, whose last survivor is **Ket**. The genetic tell is striking: **Ket speakers carry Y-haplogroup Q at high frequency — the most common New World haplogroup — uniquely in Siberia.** Inferred gene-flow age ~6,500–7,000 YBP, possibly the Na-Dene/Yeniseian split. Caveat: Yeniseian/Ket is *Central* Siberia (the Yenisei), not Northeast — so this is the structural cousin of, not identical to, what Reich named. Both are instances of the same pattern: an Asian-side language family whose closest relatives are American.

**The terrain rotation.** Post-LGM sea-level rise drowned the Bering land bridge (~11,000 years ago), converting Beringia from a walkable corridor to open ocean — the orthogonal flip from low to high friction Dave and Beta describe.

## Framework Move

**1. Diffusion curve and friction collapse operating simultaneously on one geographic feature.** The strongest move on this card. Beringia is read by *two lenses at once*: the diffusion lens (the crossing as the early-adopter leading edge of a propagating migration; non-crossers as the trailing distribution) and the friction-collapse lens (the corridor's friction state inverting when the land bridge drowns). Most cards apply the lenses sequentially; here they bear on the same feature in the same moment. Worth marking as a model instance of the fractal three-lens structure converging on a single geographic object.

**2. Orthogonal rotation: low-to-high friction freezing populations in place.** Extends the friction-collapse apparatus. The usual friction-collapse story is high→low (a technology or route opens a corridor). Beringia is the *reverse*: a corridor that closes, raising friction to near-infinity and freezing the resident distribution — both the population that never crossed and any backflow population that had recrossed. The terrain rotation doesn't move people; it *immobilizes* them, and immobilization-in-place is what preserves the signal.

**3. Backflow population isolated by barrier-formation — the Congo split in reverse.** Direct extension of the chimp-bonobo geographic-speciation material (cards 263–264). There, a barrier *forms to separate* a previously connected population. Here, a barrier *forms to isolate a backflow population that had briefly reconnected the two sides* — the mirror image. Same mechanism (barrier formation drives divergence/isolation), inverted initial condition. Strengthens the framework's claim that geographic-barrier dynamics are a general principle running in both directions.

**4. Language as the durable signature of a lost connection (linguistic robust-frontier / Wald-bomber signal).** The Eskimo-Aleut presence on the Siberian side is the *surviving, observable* signal of a connection the terrain made impossible to maintain — language as the recoverable trace, the way the Mal'ta-boy genome (card 265) is the recoverable trace of Ancient North Eurasians. The isolated language is the returning bomber; the lost web of contacts that produced it is the engine hit. Connects to card 270's outcome-not-experience boundary: the language records *that* there was a connection, not what it was like.

**5. "The terrain is patient everywhere" — framework universality, now confirmed in the Western Hemisphere.** This is the first Reich-arc card in the Americas. The same substrate logic the arc has validated across Eurasia (steppe, Bactria, India) now appears in Beringia — geographic determinism as a hemisphere-independent principle, not a Eurasian artifact of where the evidence happened to be richest.

## Calibration

1. **"After the First Americans" is a section, not a chapter title.** Reich's chapter is **Ch. 7, "In Search of Native American Ancestors."** No prose should attribute "After the First Americans" as a chapter title; it's a section/subsection. The framework move doesn't depend on it.

2. **The language is most likely Eskimo-Aleut/Yupik, but confirm which Reich named.** Two candidate phenomena exist — Eskimo-Aleut backflow (Naukan/Chukchi, *Northeast* Siberia, the better fit for Dave's "Northeast") and Dene-Yeniseian/Ket (*Central* Siberia, the more famous "language reconnects two continents" case). Worth a glance at the passage to see which Reich invoked; the framework reading holds for either.

3. **Beta's Tocharian / Tarim-mummy analogy is genetically outdated — flag it.** Beta likened the isolated Siberian language to "the Tocharian mummies in the Tarim Basin." This conflates two different things: Tocharian is a real Indo-European linguistic isolate attested in 1st-millennium-CE oasis texts, but the **Bronze Age Tarim Basin mummies (~2000 BCE) were shown by Zhang et al. 2021 (*Nature*) to be a genetically isolated *local* population descended from Ancient North Eurasians — not recent migrants and not the bearers of an incoming language.** Calling them "Tocharian mummies" is exactly the 20th-century popular conflation that aDNA overturned (cf. card 266's aDNA-overturns-old-consensus theme). The *structural* point Beta wanted (an isolate preserving the trace of a lost connection) is fine, but the Tarim mummies are a poor illustration of it — ironically they're better read as a robust-frontier ANE relict, kin to the Mal'ta material (card 265). Use the Beringian case on its own terms; drop the Tarim analogy or replace it.

4. **The Rogers diffusion-curve mapping is a productive analogy, not a literal Rogers process.** "Crossing Beringia" is a migration decision, not adoption of an innovation; the people who stayed weren't "laggards" in any sense that imputes failure or inferiority. The early-adopter/laggard framing illuminates the *shape* of the leading-edge-vs-trailing-distribution dynamic but should not be read as a value ranking — consistent with the framework's anti-great-man and anti-eugenic care. Hold the diffusion-curve reading as heuristic.

5. **Back-migration timing and direction were bidirectional and multi-wave.** The Siberia↔Americas exchange involved several pulses (First Americans out; Paleo-Eskimo movements; Neo-Eskimo/Thule; Eskimo-Aleut backflow) over thousands of years, with bidirectional admixture. The card's clean "out then back" framing is a simplification of a multi-wave bidirectional process; hold at macro level.
