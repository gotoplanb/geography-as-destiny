## The mechanism — a two-link physical chain, both links quantifiable
Elevation converts to biological productivity through two well-characterized physical laws:

1. **Lapse rate (elevation → temperature).** Air temperature falls with height. Standard-atmosphere (ISA) environmental lapse rate ≈ **6.5 °C/km ≈ 2 °C per 1,000 ft**; the **dry adiabatic** rate ≈ **9.8 °C/km ≈ 3 °C per 1,000 ft** (Dave's pilot figure — the value aviators memorize; the "density altitude" cousin is the same family). So a plateau 1,000–1,300 m above a neighboring lowland is on the order of **~6–9 °C colder** from elevation alone, before any other factor.
2. **Clausius–Clapeyron (temperature → moisture ceiling).** The saturation vapor pressure of water rises ~**7% per °C** (roughly doubling per ~10 °C). Colder air therefore has a **lower ceiling on how much water vapor it can hold** — a weaker hydrological cycle and less precipitation potential. Cold caps moisture.

Chain it: **higher elevation → colder (lapse rate) → lower atmospheric-moisture ceiling (Clausius–Clapeyron) → less precipitation potential → lower plant productivity / carrying capacity → a more marginal environment.** This is the literal physics under the framework's otherwise-metaphorical "thermodynamic gradient" — at least on the temperature/moisture axis.

**Dave's sharpening is the crux:** *colder is not the operative variable — moisture is.* Cold matters only because, via Clausius–Clapeyron, it lowers the moisture ceiling and therefore the growth ceiling. "It's higher so it's colder" is trivia; "it's higher so it's colder so it holds less water so it grows less" is a productivity mechanism.

## What it unifies — cold however caused lowers the ceiling
The same two-link physics powers two cases the framework had treated separately:

- **Temporal cooling (card 323 — the Younger Dryas).** A drop in *time* (YD cold reversal) lowers the moisture ceiling → lower wild-plant yield → shifts the foraging-vs-farming margin. The Clausius–Clapeyron kernel card 323 kept is *this same second link*, driven by temporal rather than spatial cooling.
- **Spatial elevation (card 320 — the east-high/west-low steppe).** A rise in *space* (Mongolian Plateau ~1,000–1,500 m vs. Pontic-Caspian ~0–200 m) lowers the moisture ceiling on the eastern steppe → drier, less productive, more marginal than the western steppe.

**One mechanism, two triggers.** Whether the cold comes from a climate reversal (time) or from altitude (space), the consequence is the same: *lower moisture ceiling → lower productivity.* That is a satisfying unification — the framework's "cold = marginal" intuition is really "cold = dry = low-yield," and it applies identically across time and elevation.

## Retroactive upgrade to card 320 — the elevation gradient matters, but not for the reason I first dismissed
Card 320 correctly rejected the naïve **"downhill → flow west"** reading of the steppe elevation gradient and downgraded elevation to "a modest bias." **This card supplies the real reason elevation matters, and it is *not* the downhill one:** the eastern steppe is higher → colder → drier → **less productive**, i.e. **more marginal**. That makes the eastern steppe a stronger **pump source** and the lower/warmer/wetter western steppe a more attractive **target** — a *productivity* gradient, not a *gravity* gradient. So elevation's westward relevance is real, but it runs through **carrying capacity**, not slope. And it **compounds card 319's continentality**: the eastern steppe is marginal on *two* independent physical axes — higher *variance* (continentality) **and** lower *mean productivity* (elevation → cold → dry). Both make it a pump source; neither is the "downhill" story.

## Elevation has *two* distinct framework consequences (this complements 297)
Card 297 already uses elevation — but for its **traversal-cost** consequence: crossing high terrain costs metabolic energy, so mountains are **friction sinks**. This card adds elevation's **climatic** consequence: high terrain is **colder → drier → less productive**. Same variable, two independent physical effects:
- **297:** elevation → energy cost to *cross* → friction sink / barrier.
- **324:** elevation → cold → low moisture ceiling → low *productivity* → marginal / pump source.
A mountain range is thus both hard to cross *and* a productivity desert on top; a plateau need not be hard to cross yet is still a productivity-suppressed zone. Keeping the two consequences distinct sharpens the apparatus.

## Why this matters
- **Grounds the "thermodynamics-heavy" claim in real, calculable physics.** The framework repeatedly invokes thermodynamic gradients (276/297/318/319); this card gives the temperature/moisture axis an actual, quantitative atmospheric-physics chain (lapse rate + Clausius–Clapeyron), moving it from evocative metaphor toward literal mechanism — and making it *falsifiable* (productivity should track the moisture ceiling set by elevation-driven temperature).
- **Unifies two prior cards under one mechanism** (323 temporal, 320 spatial), which is the kind of consolidation that strengthens a framework.

## Calibration / open items
1. **Elevation-cooling sets a moisture *ceiling*; it is not the *sole* cause of a region's dryness.** Actual precipitation also needs moisture *supply* (circulation, oceanic distance, rain-shadow/orographics). The eastern steppe is dry partly from elevation-cooling *and* partly from deep-interior continentality and distance from oceanic moisture (319). Claim: elevation caps the ceiling and compounds the other factors — not that elevation alone makes it dry.
2. **Get the numbers right if load-bearing.** ISA environmental lapse rate ≈ 6.5 °C/km (~2 °C/1,000 ft); dry adiabatic ≈ 9.8 °C/km (~3 °C/1,000 ft — Dave's pilot figure); Clausius–Clapeyron ≈ 7%/°C. Use the *environmental* rate for terrain-elevation temperature differences.
3. **"Density altitude" is the intuition source, not the mechanism.** The aviation concept that primed Dave's insight concerns air density and aircraft performance; the framework-relevant links are lapse rate (elevation→temp) and Clausius–Clapeyron (temp→moisture). Cite those, not density altitude, as the mechanism.
4. **Productivity depends on more than moisture** (growing-season length, soil, solar input). This card isolates the temperature→moisture→productivity link; it does not claim moisture is the only productivity input. It is one clean, quantifiable axis of the gradient.
5. **Complements, doesn't replace, 297/319.** 297 keeps elevation's traversal-cost/friction-sink role; 319 keeps continentality/variance; this card adds the elevation→moisture→productivity axis alongside them.
