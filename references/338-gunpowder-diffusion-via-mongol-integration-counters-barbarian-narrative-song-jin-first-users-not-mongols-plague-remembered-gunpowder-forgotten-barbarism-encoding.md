## The bundled claims, separated and checked
Dave/Beta bundled four claims; they have different truth-values and must not travel together.
1. **Gunpowder is a Chinese invention.** ✓ **Solid.** Tang-era alchemists (9th c.); the earliest known *written* formulas are in the Song military compendium **Wujing Zongyao (~1044)**.
2. **The first military use was during the Song — *before* the Mongols.** ✓ **Solid, and it corrects Dave's phrasing.** The **Song** fielded **fire lances (~1150)**, **thunderclap/thunderbolt bombs** (used defending Kaifeng in the 1120s), and iron-shell bombs; the **Jin** used gunpowder **thunder-crash bombs (*zhentianlei*)** and flying fire-lances **against the Mongols** at the siege of **Kaifeng in 1232.** Gunpowder weapons let the militarily weaker Song hold off stronger enemies until the Mongols finally overran them in the 1270s.
3. **The Mongols were adopters, not originators.** ✓ They **encountered** gunpowder weapons as *targets* of them (Jin bombs at Kaifeng, 1232) and **adopted** the technology the way they adopted every useful thing — by **recruiting the engineers** who made it (the systematic talent-recruitment of card 118 / the Weatherford material).
4. **The Mongol era was the vector for gunpowder's westward spread to the Islamic world and Europe.** ✓ **Majority view — but genuinely debated** (see the hedge below).

## The correction that matters — transmitters, not originators
"The Mongols were really the first to use gunpowder in warfare" is **false as stated**, and getting it right makes the framework point *cleaner*, not weaker. The first military users were the **Song and the Jin**; the Mongols first met gunpowder on the *receiving* end (Kaifeng, 1232) and then absorbed it. So the reframe is not "the barbarians secretly invented guns" — it is the stronger and more defensible "**the Mongols were the integration layer that moved an existing Chinese military technology across Eurasia.**" Originator vs. scaling/transmission is the same distinction the framework draws for the wheel and for farming (168/268/169): the Mongols are the **amplification-and-transmission zone**, not the source.

## The evidence-strength hedge — majority but contested; what the framework actually needs
The westward-transmission claim is the **consensus**, and the timing is suggestive: gunpowder recipes and the first gunpowder weapons appear in **Europe, the Islamic world, and India late in the 13th century — after contact with Mongol armies** — and **Hasan al-Rammah's Arabic treatise (~1280)** records the ingredients as Chinese in origin (saltpetre as "**Chinese snow**," rockets as "**Chinese arrows**"). **But** respected historians (**J. J. Saunders, Kate Raphael**) **minimize or deny** the Mongols' role as either heavy *users* of gunpowder in the western campaigns or *transmitters* of the knowledge; the diffusion is hard to track (terminology ambiguity), and the Mongols likely did not travel with much powder and were reluctant to share the recipe. **Do not overstate "the Mongols brought gunpowder to Europe" as settled fact.** The safe, framework-sufficient claim: **the Mongol-era Eurasian integration was the transmission *window*** — whether via Mongol armies specifically or via the general Silk-Road connectivity the Pax Mongolica (095) opened, gunpowder crossed west during it, with Chinese-named ingredients as the fingerprint. The framework point below **does not depend on the contested "Mongol armies carried it" version.**

## The framework payoff — plague remembered, gunpowder forgotten (barbarism-encoding, 256)
This is the real contribution, and it is sound. The **same** Mongol-era integration transmitted **two** things west: the **Black Death** (269) and **gunpowder**. The record remembers the first vividly — pyramids of skulls, rivers of blood, plague-bearing horde — because trauma generates durable sender-side documentation (256's barbarism-encoding; 228's what-gets-breezed-past). It largely *forgets* the second, or files it elsewhere, because a century of technology transfer generates routine records nobody preserves as literature. **The destructive transmission is over-remembered; the generative transmission is breezed past — and both rode the identical channel.** That asymmetry is not a fact about the Mongols; it is a fact about the *encoding*. Recovering the gunpowder transmission is the same move the book already makes with the **paper diffusion after the Battle of Talas** (the named battle remembered, the technology transfer footnoted) and with the Pax Mongolica's postal system forgotten behind the terror.

## Bidirectional integration engine — gunpowder west, Muslim trebuchet east
The strongest anti-barbarian datum: the transfer ran **both ways** through the Mongol system. While Chinese gunpowder moved west, **Muslim counterweight-trebuchet engineers** (the *huihui pao*) were moved **east** to break the Song fortress of **Xiangyang (1273)**. The Mongols were a **technology-integration engine** — pulling siege expertise from Persia to China and pushing gunpowder from China toward the west — which is the precise opposite of the culture-destroying-horde image. Same apparatus, read accurately (256's intellectual-honesty commitment).

## Diffusion lens — the compass parallel (two-mode)
Gunpowder's spread is a clean parallel to the **compass** (236) and **paper**: a Chinese invention that diffused west in the classic pattern — a long overland/relay phase and then acceleration through a period of intensified connectivity (here, Mongol integration). Like the compass, it exhibits a **pre-recognition / re-encoding gap**: the same substance is an alchemical/incendiary curiosity in one context and a decisive military technology in another; adoption starts at *recognition*, not invention. The transmission is **process-knowledge**, not just artifact (168) — recipes (proportions of saltpetre/sulphur/charcoal) had to travel, which is why al-Rammah's *written* recipe is the telling evidence.

## Why this matters
- **Extends the Mongols-as-connectors reframe** (095/118/256) with a second concrete transmission (after the Pax Mongolica's commercial/postal integration): gunpowder, the technology that would remake the world, crossed Eurasia through the very system the barbarian narrative erases.
- **Pairs cleanly with the plague card (269):** two transmissions, one channel, opposite memory — a textbook barbarism-encoding demonstration.
- **Models the correction discipline:** the flashy version ("Mongols invented/first-used gunpowder") is wrong; the defensible version (integration-layer transmission) is stronger.

## Calibration / open items
1. **Correct "Mongols first used gunpowder in war" everywhere → Song/Jin were first; Mongols were adopters/transmitters.** Non-negotiable; the Jin bombed the Mongols at Kaifeng (1232) before the Mongols used it themselves.
2. **Hedge the westward vector.** Majority view, real dissent (Saunders, Raphael). Frame as "the Mongol-era integration was the transmission window," not "Mongol armies carried gunpowder to Europe." The framework payoff (asymmetric memory) does not need the contested version.
3. **Do not claim a single vector.** Gunpowder plausibly also moved via the Islamic world / Silk Road independent of direct Mongol military contact; the Mongol integration is the *window*, not necessarily the sole conduit.
4. **Verify Cunliffe's exact framing** of gunpowder in the closing chapter (Dave's listening) before prose; the underlying dates (Wujing Zongyao 1044; fire lance ~1150; Kaifeng 1232; al-Rammah ~1280; Xiangyang 1273) are well established, verify each if used.
5. **Bidirectional detail (huihui pao east / gunpowder west)** is the anti-barbarian crux — keep it; it is the cleanest single expression of the integration-engine reading.
