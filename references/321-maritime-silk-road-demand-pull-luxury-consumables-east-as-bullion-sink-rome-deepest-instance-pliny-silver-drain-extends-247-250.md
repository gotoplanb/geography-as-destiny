## Move 1 — Demand-pull: luxury consumables after luxury durables
The framework reads trade mostly from the *supply/transmission* side (corridors, friction collapse, who holds the chokepoint). Dave's spice observation adds the **demand** side, and names a real progression: a **wealthy, concentrated-surplus sedentary empire, having saturated on luxury *durables* (gems, precious metals, fine textiles), escalates into luxury *consumables* (pepper, cinnamon, incense)** — goods that are literally consumed and must be *re-bought every year*, generating a permanent, inelastic import appetite. This is a demand structure only a dense sedentary surplus civilization produces (the steppe pastoralist has no equivalent hunger). Rome is the exemplar: black pepper as an everyday elite staple, whole cookbooks (Apicius) built on eastern spice. The demand-pull is the counterpart to the corridor's supply-push — and it is what *charges* the drain in Move 2.

## Move 2 (the jewel) — the recurring "East as the world's bullion sink," with Rome as the deepest instance
Because the East (India, then China) produced the luxury consumables the wealthy West craved **and wanted little in return**, the trade ran chronically *unbalanced*, settled in **precious metal flowing east**. This is a recurring, trans-historical pattern, and the archive already holds its later instances — this card supplies the earliest and deepest:

- **Rome → India/China (1st c. CE) — the deepest instance (NEW).** Once Hippalus's crews learned the **monsoon winds** (~45–62 CE; the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*), direct Red Sea–India voyages opened, and Roman **gold and silver bled east** for pepper, spices, gems, and transshipped Chinese silk. **Pliny the Elder**: *"there is no year in which India does not drain the Roman Empire of a hundred million sesterces"* and India is *"the sink of the world's gold."* (Roman coin hoards in South India confirm it.)
- **American silver → China (1565–1815) — card 247.** The Manila Galleon pours Potosí/Mexican silver into China; the same eastern sink, now fed from the New World; couples into the late-Ming fiscal crisis.
- **The West violently *reverses* it — the Opium Wars (1839–60s), card 250.** Britain faces the identical structural deficit (China wants tea/silk/porcelain; Britain has nothing China wants → silver drains east) and **weaponizes opium** to invert the flow. Card 250's "extraction disguised as exchange / dark mirror of the Silk Road" is, in this longer view, **the violent Western correction of an ~1,800-year-old structural trade asymmetry** — the deficit Pliny first complained about, finally reversed by force.

**The pattern:** the East was the **terminal sink of global bullion for nearly two millennia** because it made what the West wanted and wanted little back. Rome's silver, then America's silver, all flowed toward the same sink; the Opium Wars are the moment the West stopped paying and took instead. Seeing Rome as the first node reveals 247 and 250 not as two China-centric episodes but as **two late chapters of one trans-historical dynamic**.

**Framework reading (ties to the gradient apparatus, 297/318/319):** bullion flows *down the demand gradient* toward the production zone of the desired good, exactly as populations flow down pressure gradients. Trade imbalance is the monetary analog of the thermodynamic flow the framework already uses — the specie is the medium, the luxury-good production zone is the sink. And the Opium Wars show what happens when the deficit party has the *power-projection* margin (card 312) to refuse the gradient and reverse it by force rather than keep paying.

## Why this matters
- **Adds the demand-pull driver** the trade cards lacked — the consumption appetite of a saturated sedentary surplus as a *cause* of long-distance trade, complementing the corridor/supply reading.
- **Deepens the silver-drain pattern by ~1,600 years**, turning two China-centric cards (247/250) into one trans-historical dynamic with Rome as origin node — a stronger, more general claim.
- **Anti-great-man dividend:** frames the Opium Wars as the structural correction of a millennial trade asymmetry, not a moral drama or a British-genius scheme (sharpens 250).
