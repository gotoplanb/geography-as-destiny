# Scott — Against the Grain

**James C. Scott, *Against the Grain: A Deep History of the Earliest States* (Yale University Press, 2017)**

Scott (1936–2024) was Sterling Professor of Political Science and Anthropology at Yale and the founder of its Agrarian Studies program. His career ran on a single question — how ordinary people evade, absorb and subvert the states that try to administer them — worked out across *Seeing Like a State* (1998), *The Art of Not Being Governed* (2009) and this book.

## What it argues

The conventional sequence — agriculture produces surplus, surplus produces cities, cities produce states, and all of this is progress — is wrong on the timing and wrong on the mechanism. Thousands of years separate the domestication of plants from the appearance of states, which is an embarrassment for the standard story. Scott's answer is that what makes a state possible is not surplus but *appropriable* surplus.

Cereal grains are uniquely taxable because they are "visible, divisible, assessable, storable, transportable, and rationable." Above all they ripen at a known moment, above ground, all at once, so a tax collector can stand in the field and take a share. Roots and tubers are nearly appropriation-proof: they keep in the ground for up to two years and are dug piecemeal as needed, so there is no harvest to assess. Scott's distinction is between **state crops** and **state-evading crops**.

The corollary, developed at length in *The Art of Not Being Governed*, is that early states were small, fragile, and frequently escaped from. Being outside a state was often the better deal, and "barbarians" were frequently refugees rather than primitives.

## What we use it for

- **Chapter 9 (The Parallel Conquests) — naming a mechanism the chapter already runs.** The bison argument is Scott's thesis in American dress: *"The American administrative frustration with the Comanche was never about resource management capacity. It was about sedentarization."* A mobile pastoral economy on a migratory resource is un-appropriable in Scott's exact sense. Destroying the resource base was the instrument of conversion to an appropriable one. See card 345.
- **Chapter 4 (The River Spine) — the surplus-to-class-structure link.** The chapter derives class structure from river energy. Scott adds the missing term: it is not surplus that stratifies, it is surplus someone can count.
- **Chapter 6 (Silk Road) — the relay without the state.** New Guinea had relay trade, prestige goods and accumulated surplus in pigs, and produced no states. Scott supplies why. See card 344.
- **A third settlement axis.** Cards 299 and 301 give food *density* and fixed-vs-mobile resource *geometry*. Appropriability is independent of both.
- **The anti-great-man and anti-eugenic commitments (283, 284, 260).** Scott's mechanism turns on crop biology and legibility, never on the capability of the governed.

## Limitations

*(Provisional — these are the standard criticisms, not yet Dave's.)*

- Scott is a polemicist by temperament and the book is an argument, not a survey. Expect the evidence to be selected.
- Archaeologists have pushed back on the periodization and on how uniformly the grain/state link holds; check whether he engages the counter-cases or routes around them.
- The anarchist commitment is explicit and load-bearing. That is not disqualifying — this project has its own explicit frame — but it means the thesis and the politics arrive together and should be separated deliberately.
- He works mostly in Mesopotamia and Southeast Asia. Whether the mechanism generalizes to the Nile, the Yellow River and Mesoamerica is exactly the sort of thing this project should test rather than assume.
