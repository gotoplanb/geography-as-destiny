# Appendix D: Chronology

The book is organized by argument, not by time. Each chapter starts from a structural claim and moves across geography to demonstrate it. For readers who prefer a chronological entry point — or who want to verify that a single argument actually spans the millennia it claims to — this appendix lists the dated events the reference cards engage with, ordered from deepest prehistory to the present.

Not every notecard appears here. Cards that are purely methodological — Shannon encoding theory, the Wald bomber correction, the framework's own apparatus — have no temporal anchor and are omitted. What remains is the historical evidence the framework was built against.

Card references are given as `(NNN)` matching filenames in `references/`. Where multiple cards engage the same event, they are listed together. Dates are given in BCE/CE convention. For archaeological cultures, climate horizons, and reconstructed language phases, conventional date ranges are used; precise dating is contested in many cases and the entries flag the major ranges rather than picking a winner.

## Deep Time and the Out-of-Africa Window

- **~1.5–2 million years ago** — Chimpanzee–bonobo divergence after Congo River formation; foundational illustration of geography producing genetic divergence in real biological time (263, 279)
- **~210,000 years ago** — Anatomically modern human remains in Greece, pushing African exit window earlier than long assumed (259)
- **~180,000 years ago** — Anatomically modern human remains in the Israeli Levant (259)
- **~150,000 years ago** — Coalescence of mitochondrial Eve (259)
- **~50,000 years ago** — Out-of-Africa expansion proper, coincident with a Saharan green pulse; modern human–Neanderthal admixture across non-African populations (259, 261)
- **~50,000+ years ago** — Altai mixing zone hosts archaic and modern human populations including Denisovans (261)
- **~45,000–40,000 BCE** — Climate oscillations drive Neanderthal contraction (263)
- **~30,000 years ago** — KhoeSan northwest/southeast genetic split across the Kalahari; mobility threshold of foragers versus pastoralists (279)
- **~24,000 years ago** — Mal'ta boy burial near Lake Baikal; Ancient North Eurasian ancestry that later contributes to both Yamnaya and First Americans (265)
- **~20,000 years ago** — Last Glacial Maximum; Bering land bridge open; KhoeSan in isolation phase (272, 274)
- **~18,000 BCE** — Bering land bridge configuration at LGM peak (274)
- **~13,000+ years ago** — First Americans cross from Beringia (272)
- **~11,000 years ago** — Post-LGM sea-level rise drowns the Bering land bridge; later Eskimo-Aleut reflux preserves linguistic signature of the lost connection (272)

## Holocene Foundations (c. 11,000 – 4500 BCE)

- **c. 13,000–9,700 BCE** — Natufian semi-sedentary foragers in the Levant, exploiting wild cereals before domestication; thermodynamic (fixed-resource) sedentism, not yet agriculture (301, 323)
- **c. 11,000 BCE** — Aegean Mesolithic seafaring: Melos obsidian at Franchthi Cave (~80 nautical miles of open-water crossing) — seafaring as an embedded lifeway, earlier than the western-Mediterranean colonizations (300)
- **c. 10,900–9,700 BCE** — Younger Dryas cold reversal at the Pleistocene–Holocene boundary; the classic "aridity forced Levantine agriculture" reading is contested, and the framework holds it as a thermodynamic incentive-nudge at an already-primed crossroads, not a clean forcing (301, 323)
- **c. 11,000–5,000 BCE** — Green Sahara; the desert is savanna with lakes and rivers, supporting dispersed nomadic populations (045, 047)
- **c. 9,000 BCE** — Corsica reached (Araguina-Sennola); western-Mediterranean island colonization as constraint-bred maritime competence (300)
- **c. 10,000–8,000 BCE** — Plant and animal domestication in the Fertile Crescent (169, 266)
- **c. 8,000 BCE** — Green Sahara approximate persistence in northern Africa (274)
- **c. 7,000 BCE** — Anatolian farmer expansion begins into the Balkans and southern Europe (169, 170)
- **c. 5,600–5,000 BCE** — Cardial/Impressed-Ware Neolithic spreads along the Mediterranean sea-corridor; a maritime-Neolithic friction collapse (304, 305)
- **c. 5,500–4,900 BCE** — Linearbandkeramik (LBK) rapid colonization across the loess belt, driven by a Spondylus-shell prestige-goods economy, not frontier psychology (302)
- **c. 5,300–3,950 BCE** — Ertebølle Baltic foragers resist agriculture for millennia while in contact with farmers; a ~4000 BCE climate shift later moves the foraging-vs-farming margin toward adoption (306)
- **c. 6,500–5,500 BCE** — Saharan pump operating; population pulses out of Africa during humid phase (188)
- **c. 6,500–3,500 BCE** — Old Europe (Cucuteni-Tripolye) cultural complex; large agglomerated settlements before steppe pressure (190, 223)
- **c. 5,500 years ago** — Final Saharan desertification consolidates (047)
- **c. 5,000 years ago** — Egyptian civilization emerges along the Nile (047)
- **c. 4,900–3,500 BCE** — Khvalynsk culture on the middle Volga; predecessor to Yamnaya (196)
- **c. 4,650 BCE** — Vinča Pločnik earliest tin bronze in southeast Europe (181)
- **c. 4,500 BCE** — Sredny Stog culture; seasonal trade fairs at the Old Europe–steppe frontier; earliest documented bit-wear horse-management evidence (192, 193, 196, 204)
- **c. 4,500–3,500 BCE** — Phase 1 PIE federation crystallization on the Pontic-Caspian steppe; Old Europe–steppe exchange-then-conflict cycle (194, 196)
- **c. 4,500–2,500 BCE** — Proto-Indo-European reconstructed substrate (159)

## A Note on What's Missing

This list is asymmetric. The Bronze Age substrate has dozens of entries because the cards that engage it are doing the patient work of reconstructing prehistoric mechanisms from archaeology, ancient DNA, and reconstructed language. The 20th century has fewer entries than its density warrants — the framework largely treats it as a decoder for older patterns rather than a primary object of study. The 21st century appears mostly as the publication dates of the scholarship the book engages.

What the chronology is good for: tracing how a single argument — that geography sets the probability distribution of historical outcomes — pulls evidence from across the millennia and finds the same shape repeating. What it is not good for: substituting for the chapters. The dates are anchors. The arguments are in the prose.
